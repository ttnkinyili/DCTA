# Modelling Trust Decay Over Time: Exponential Evidence Discounting and Practical Implementation Through Sliding Windows and Forgetting Factors

Trust, in the context of continuous access control within heterogeneous enterprise networks, is not a permanent property bestowed at the moment of authentication. It is an ephemeral, volatile quantity whose validity degrades with the passage of time. The recognition of this temporality — that an authentication event verified thirty minutes ago carries substantially less evidentiary weight than one verified thirty seconds ago — constitutes one of the defining departures of Zero Trust Architecture from its perimeter-based predecessors. Perimeter security and static Role-Based Access Control treat authentication as a binary, timeless gate: an entity is either authenticated or it is not, and the temporal distance from the authentication event has no bearing on its continued validity. This timelessness creates the fundamental "implicit trust period" vulnerability — a window during which a compromised credential, hijacked session, or degraded device continues to operate under the authority of an increasingly stale authentication signal (Rose et al., 2020). Modelling the decay of trust over time is therefore not a refinement of access control but a structural prerequisite for any architecture that claims to implement continuous verification.

The mathematical formulation of trust decay begins with a deceptively simple question: at what rate should the evidentiary weight of a trust observation diminish? Two canonical families of decay functions dominate the literature — linear and exponential — and their behavioural differences carry profound architectural implications.

Linear decay models trust depreciation as a constant-rate process. A trust score established at session initiation decreases by equal increments per unit time, reaching zero at the end of a predefined session window. Formally, the decay weight at elapsed time *t* within a session of duration *T*_session is expressed as *D*(*t*) = max(0, 1 − *t*/*T*_session). The simplicity of this formulation is also its limitation. At the midpoint of a thirty-minute enterprise session, the initial authentication signal still retains fifty per cent of its original weight — a degree of residual authority that is dangerously disproportionate to the evidentiary value of a fifteen-minute-old credential in a volatile network environment. Modern adversaries can hijack a session endpoint, perform credential relay attacks, or computationally compromise a device in a matter of seconds (IBM Security, 2024). A decay function that still grants half-authority to a fifteen-minute-old authentication event creates precisely the prolonged implicit trust period that continuous verification architectures are designed to eliminate.

Exponential decay resolves this architectural deficiency by front-loading the depreciation curve. The exponential decay weight *W*(*t*) = *e*^(−λ·*t*), where λ is the decay rate constant, produces a steeply convex degradation path in which the vast majority of evidentiary weight is shed within the first few minutes of a session. At λ = 0.1 and *t* = 5 minutes, the residual weight has already dropped to approximately 0.61; by *t* = 15, it has fallen to 0.22; and by *t* = 30, it retains a mere 0.05 — less than five per cent of its original authority. The decay rate constant λ serves as a tuneable architectural parameter: higher values produce more aggressive depreciation, suitable for high-security environments where freshness is paramount; lower values permit gentler curves for stable, low-risk sessions (Josang, 2016). This tunability enables the same mathematical framework to govern both short-term sessions of thirty minutes and long-term behavioural baselines of forty-eight hours, differing only in the parametrisation of λ.

The superiority of exponential decay for modelling trust freshness is grounded in information-theoretic principles that extend well beyond the cybersecurity domain. In signal processing, the exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA) has long been recognised as the optimal estimator for non-stationary processes because it assigns geometrically decreasing weights to older observations, ensuring that the estimator tracks the current state of the process rather than its historical mean (Hunter, 1986). In Bayesian inference, the exponential discounting of prior evidence is the canonical mechanism for preventing "belief inertia" — the pathological condition in which an agent's posterior distribution becomes dominated by accumulated historical evidence to the point where new observations cannot shift it (Kulhavy & Zarrop, 1993). In the specific context of Dempster-Shafer evidence theory, which provides the mathematical foundation for the trust fusion architecture under discussion, temporal discounting takes the form of mass redistribution: a proportion of each evidence source's committed belief mass is transferred to the vacuous mass *m*(Θ), representing a controlled injection of uncertainty that prevents stale evidence from dominating the fused belief state (Mercier et al., 2012). This discounting operation preserves the structural integrity of the Dempster-Shafer combination rule while acknowledging that the reliability of evidence degrades with temporal distance from the observation event.

The formal mechanism by which exponential discounting operates within the Dempster-Shafer framework deserves careful explication, as it is the mathematical bridge between the abstract concept of trust decay and the concrete operation of the trust fusion engine. Given a body of evidence characterised by a basic probability assignment (BPA) *m*(*A*) for each hypothesis *A* ⊆ Θ, temporal discounting applies a decay factor α(*t*) = *e*^(−λ·*t*) that redistributes a fraction (1 − α) of each committed mass to the frame of discernment Θ. The discounted BPA becomes *m*_α(*A*) = α · *m*(*A*) for all *A* ⊂ Θ and *m*_α(Θ) = 1 − α · (1 − *m*(Θ)). As *t* increases, α → 0, and the discounted evidence converges to the vacuous mass function *m*(Θ) = 1, representing total ignorance. This asymptotic behaviour is epistemologically appropriate: evidence whose temporal distance from the present exceeds the system's memory horizon should contribute no committed belief to the fused output, leaving the fusion engine entirely dependent on fresh observations. Smets (2000) established the theoretical foundations of this discounting operation within the Transferable Belief Model, demonstrating that it preserves the associativity and commutativity of Dempster's combination rule while providing a principled mechanism for encoding source reliability — in this case, the "reliability" of evidence as a function of its age.

The practical implementation of temporal decay in operational trust management systems requires two complementary engineering mechanisms: sliding windows and forgetting factors. These are not alternative approaches but synergistic components of a unified temporal management architecture.

A sliding window defines the temporal horizon over which the trust evaluation engine considers evidence. Evidence older than the window boundary is either discarded entirely (hard windowing) or subjected to accelerated discounting (soft windowing). The choice of window length is architecturally significant. A short-term window — typically thirty minutes for enterprise session management — captures the immediate behavioural context of the current session: the devices being used, the resources being accessed, the network conditions prevailing, and the anomaly signals detected since authentication (Chen & Wang, 2025). A long-term window — extending to forty-eight hours or more — captures the entity's sustained behavioural baseline, providing the historical inertia against which current deviations are measured. The dual-window architecture is critical for distinguishing between *acute* trust signals (a sudden anomaly in the current session) and *chronic* trust patterns (a gradual degradation in compliance posture over multiple sessions). A single window, regardless of its length, cannot serve both purposes: a short window lacks the memory to identify slow-moving threats, while a long window dampens the system's responsiveness to immediate anomalies (Alsubhi et al., 2024).

The sliding window mechanism also addresses a computational imperative that is frequently overlooked in the theoretical trust literature. In heterogeneous enterprise networks comprising thousands of devices, each generating continuous streams of trust-relevant telemetry — authentication events, device posture assessments, traffic anomaly scores, application behavioural metrics — the trust evaluation engine cannot maintain and process an unbounded history for every entity. The sliding window imposes a finite, architecturally bounded memory horizon that constrains the computational and storage requirements of the trust engine to a function of the window length multiplied by the number of monitored entities, rather than growing unboundedly with operational time (Ahmed et al., 2024). This bounded memory property is not merely an implementation convenience; it is a structural requirement for deployability in resource-constrained environments where the trust evaluation engine must operate at the network edge with limited computational budgets.

Within the sliding window, the forgetting factor governs the relative weighting of observations as a function of their age. The forgetting factor is the discrete-time analogue of the continuous exponential decay rate: at each evaluation epoch, the weight of each prior observation is multiplied by a factor α ∈ (0, 1), producing a geometrically declining weight sequence α⁰, α¹, α², ..., αⁿ for the *n* observations within the window, ordered from most recent to oldest. A forgetting factor of α = 0.95, applied at one-minute evaluation intervals within a thirty-minute window, assigns the oldest observation a relative weight of 0.95²⁹ ≈ 0.23 — less than one-quarter the weight of the most recent observation. A more aggressive factor of α = 0.90 reduces the oldest observation to 0.90²⁹ ≈ 0.047, less than five per cent. The forgetting factor thus encodes the architectural judgement about how rapidly the system should transition from relying on historical evidence to relying on current observations — the "freshness premium" that distinguishes Zero Trust's continuous verification from the timeless authentication of perimeter-based systems (Robbins et al., 2025).

The interaction between sliding windows and forgetting factors produces a composite temporal management architecture with three distinct operational phases. During the *initialisation phase* — the first few minutes following authentication — the short-term window contains minimal historical evidence, and the trust score is dominated by the authentication signal itself and the initial device posture assessment. The forgetting factor has little effect because the window contains few observations. During the *handover phase* — typically minutes five through fifteen — exponential discounting rapidly devalues the initial authentication signal, forcing the fusion engine to shift its reliance from the one-time credential verification to the accumulated short-term behavioural evidence: access patterns, data volumes, API call frequencies, and network anomaly scores. This phase represents the critical architectural handoff from "verified identity" to "observed behaviour" as the primary basis for trust (Liu et al., 2023). During the *maturity phase* — the remainder of the session — the short-term window is fully populated, the forgetting factor has established a stable recency-weighted distribution of evidence, and the trust score reflects a dynamically weighted composite of recent behaviour, device posture, and long-term historical inertia from the secondary window. Any anomalous deviation in observed behaviour during this phase produces an immediate and proportional reduction in the trust score, because the forgetting factor ensures that recent anomalous observations dominate the decayed historical baseline.

This three-phase lifecycle has a direct mathematical relationship to the Dempster-Shafer fusion engine that aggregates trust evidence across the Zero Trust pillars. During the initialisation phase, the fusion engine operates with high vacuous mass — substantial portions of the mass function are assigned to Θ, representing the engine's honest acknowledgement that it does not yet have sufficient behavioural evidence to commit belief. As the session progresses through the handover and maturity phases, the increasing volume of within-window evidence reduces the vacuous mass, and the fused belief function increasingly concentrates on specific trust hypotheses. If the evidence is consistent and non-anomalous, belief concentrates on the "trustworthy" hypothesis, and the trust score rises toward the full-access threshold. If the evidence is conflicting — for example, normal application behaviour but anomalous network traffic — the Dempster-Shafer combination rule produces elevated conflict coefficients that moderate the fused belief, and the trust score settles in the constrained-access zone between the full-access and denial thresholds (Shafer, 1976; Liu et al., 2023). The forgetting factor ensures that this conflict resolution is dominated by *recent* evidence rather than *historical* evidence, preventing a long history of benign behaviour from masking a current anomalous event.

The selection of decay parameters — the rate constant λ for continuous systems, the forgetting factor α for discrete systems, and the window lengths for short-term and long-term horizons — is not an arbitrary configuration choice but an empirically calibrated architectural decision. Excessively aggressive decay (high λ, low α, short windows) produces a system that is hyper-reactive to transient fluctuations, generating frequent false-positive trust score drops that degrade user experience and impose unnecessary re-authentication burdens. Insufficiently aggressive decay (low λ, high α, long windows) produces a system that is inertia-dominated, slow to respond to genuine threats, and vulnerable to the same implicit trust period that characterises static access control (Alsubhi et al., 2024). The optimal parametrisation lies at the intersection of two constraints: the system must be sensitive enough to detect and respond to genuine anomalies within a threat-relevant time window — typically seconds to minutes for session hijacking, minutes to hours for credential compromise — while remaining stable enough to avoid oscillatory trust score behaviour that would render the access control system operationally unusable.

Empirical calibration of these parameters requires domain-specific knowledge of the threat landscape, the volatility of the monitored signals, and the operational tolerance for false positives and false negatives. In high-security environments such as financial trading platforms or healthcare record systems, shorter windows and more aggressive forgetting factors are justified because the cost of a false-negative (permitting a compromised session) vastly exceeds the cost of a false-positive (requiring re-authentication). In lower-security environments with stable, predictable workloads, longer windows and gentler decay curves reduce user friction without materially increasing risk (Chen & Wang, 2025). The tunability of both the exponential decay function and the forgetting factor — through their respective rate constants — enables a single architectural framework to accommodate this spectrum of operational requirements without structural modification, varying only in parametric configuration.

The temporal decay mechanisms described above — exponential discounting, sliding windows, and forgetting factors — produce a continuous, dynamically evolving trust score for every monitored entity. However, a continuous score is operationally meaningless without a decision framework that translates it into concrete access control actions. This is the function of trust thresholds: the architectural mechanism by which the continuous output of the trust fusion engine is mapped to discrete, enforceable access decisions.

## Trust Thresholds for Access Decisions

The concept of a trust threshold represents the most consequential departure from the binary access logic that characterises both perimeter-based security and static Role-Based Access Control. In traditional architectures, access is a dichotomy: an entity is either inside the perimeter (trusted) or outside it (untrusted); a user either possesses the requisite role (access granted) or does not (access denied). This binary logic provides no intermediate state — there is no mechanism for granting partial, constrained, or monitored access to entities whose trustworthiness is ambiguous, uncertain, or in transition. The introduction of trust thresholds replaces this binary gate with a graduated, multi-tiered access continuum in which the level of access granted is proportional to the entity's current trust score, and the thresholds separating access tiers are themselves dynamically calibrated to reflect the prevailing threat environment (NIST, 2022; Alder, 2025).

The architectural design of a threshold-based access control system requires the specification of at least two boundary values that partition the continuous trust score range into three operationally distinct zones. The upper threshold, τ_full, defines the minimum trust score required for full, unrestricted access to the requested resource. An entity whose trust score exceeds τ_full has demonstrated — through the aggregated, temporally weighted evidence evaluated by the Dempster-Shafer fusion engine — a sufficiently high degree of trustworthiness across all relevant Zero Trust pillars (device posture, network context, application behaviour, data sensitivity, and user profile) to warrant unrestricted access. The lower threshold, τ_deny, defines the maximum trust score at which access is categorically denied. An entity whose score falls below τ_deny has failed to provide sufficient evidence of trustworthiness, and the policy enforcement point must block the access request entirely, potentially triggering a step-up authentication challenge or security incident response workflow (Alsubhi et al., 2024). Between these two boundaries lies the constrained access zone — the architecturally novel region that has no analogue in binary access control systems. Entities whose trust scores fall within this intermediate band receive limited, risk-proportionate access: they may be permitted to read but not write, to access non-sensitive resources but not classified ones, or to operate under enhanced monitoring with reduced session durations and more frequent re-evaluation intervals (Ferrara, 2024).

This three-tier architecture — full access, constrained access, and denial — is not merely an administrative convenience but a direct mathematical consequence of the Dempster-Shafer evidence framework that underlies the trust fusion engine. When the fused belief function strongly supports the "trustworthy" hypothesis with low vacuous mass and low conflict, the resulting trust score exceeds τ_full, and the system has high confidence that unrestricted access is justified. When the fused belief function strongly supports the "untrustworthy" hypothesis or exhibits very high conflict coefficients indicating irreconcilable evidence, the score falls below τ_deny, and the system has high confidence that access should be blocked. The intermediate zone corresponds precisely to the epistemic condition of *genuine uncertainty* — when the evidence is insufficient, partially conflicting, or temporally degraded to the point where the fusion engine cannot commit belief strongly to either hypothesis (Shafer, 1976; Liu et al., 2023). Rather than forcing this uncertainty into a binary decision — which would produce either dangerous false negatives or disruptive false positives — the threshold architecture routes uncertain sessions into a constrained access tier where the entity can continue to operate under observation while the fusion engine accumulates additional evidence to resolve the ambiguity. This approach operationalises the Dempster-Shafer framework's unique capacity to explicitly represent "I don't know" through the vacuous mass function, translating epistemic uncertainty directly into an access control action that is proportionate to the system's actual degree of knowledge.

The relationship between trust decay and threshold crossing is the dynamic heart of the continuous verification architecture. As temporal decay progressively reduces the evidentiary weight of the initial authentication signal, the trust score migrates downward through the access tiers unless countervailing fresh evidence — continued benign behaviour, successful step-up authentication challenges, stable device posture — arrests or reverses the decline. This creates a natural, mathematically driven pressure toward re-verification: an entity that was granted full access at session initiation will, through the inexorable operation of exponential decay alone, eventually cross below τ_full into the constrained zone, and ultimately below τ_deny into denial, unless it continuously provides fresh evidence of trustworthiness. The rate at which this migration occurs is governed by the same decay parameters (λ, α) discussed in the preceding analysis, ensuring architectural coherence between the temporal management and access decision subsystems. In stable, non-anomalous sessions, the influx of benign behavioural evidence through the sliding window offsets the temporal decay, and the trust score stabilises within the full-access zone. In sessions exhibiting anomalous behaviour, the combination of temporal decay and negative evidence produces accelerated downward migration, triggering constrained access or denial within minutes rather than hours (Robbins et al., 2025).

A critical engineering consideration in threshold-based systems is the prevention of *oscillatory behaviour* — the pathological condition in which an entity's trust score fluctuates around a threshold boundary, causing rapid alternation between access tiers. Such oscillation is operationally disruptive, degrading user experience through unpredictable access interruptions and generating excessive load on the policy enforcement infrastructure. The standard engineering solution is *hysteresis* — the introduction of asymmetric transition boundaries such that crossing from a higher tier to a lower tier requires the trust score to fall below the threshold by a defined margin δ_down, while re-entering the higher tier requires the score to exceed the threshold by a corresponding margin δ_up (Muñoz-González et al., 2023). This asymmetry ensures that transient fluctuations in the trust score do not trigger tier transitions, while sustained declines or recoveries produce stable, decisive transitions. The hysteresis margins δ_down and δ_up are themselves tuneable parameters, calibrated to the volatility of the monitored signals and the operational tolerance for access tier instability.

The dynamic calibration of the thresholds themselves — not merely the trust scores evaluated against them — is an essential feature that distinguishes the proposed architecture from static threshold systems. In a dynamic threat environment, the definition of "sufficient trustworthiness" is not a fixed quantity but varies with the organisation's threat posture. During routine operations with no active threat intelligence alerts, the thresholds may be set at relatively permissive levels (e.g., τ_full = 0.7, τ_deny = 0.3), minimising user friction and accommodating the natural variability of trust scores across the device and user population. When threat intelligence indicates an active campaign targeting the organisation — for example, a phishing wave, a known vulnerability in a widely deployed device class, or a detected lateral movement attempt — the thresholds may be dynamically elevated (e.g., τ_full = 0.85, τ_deny = 0.5), requiring substantially higher trust scores for the same level of access and automatically downgrading entities whose scores, while previously sufficient, no longer meet the elevated standard (Chen & Wang, 2025). This dynamic threshold recalibration can be automated through integration with Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platforms and threat intelligence feeds, enabling the access control system to respond to environmental changes at machine speed rather than requiring manual policy updates (Taherdoost, 2022).

The interaction between multi-pillar trust evaluation and threshold-based access decisions also enables *resource-differentiated* thresholds — the specification of different threshold values for different resource categories. A request to access a publicly available knowledge base may require a trust score of only 0.4 for full access, while a request to modify a financial database record may require 0.9. This resource-sensitivity differentiation recognises that the consequence of a false negative (granting access to an untrustworthy entity) varies enormously across resources, and that a uniform threshold across all resources would be either excessively restrictive for low-sensitivity operations or dangerously permissive for high-sensitivity ones. The specification of per-resource or per-resource-class thresholds is an architectural decision that maps directly to the organisation's data classification framework and regulatory compliance requirements, providing a formal mechanism for encoding risk appetite into access control policy (NIST, 2022; Ferrara, 2024).

Furthermore, the threshold framework accommodates *temporal threshold adjustment* within a single session. During the initialisation phase described in the preceding section — when the fusion engine operates with high vacuous mass and limited behavioural evidence — the effective threshold for full access may be temporarily relaxed to accommodate the inherent uncertainty of the early-session trust score, provided that the initial authentication was sufficiently strong (e.g., multi-factor authentication with hardware token). As the session progresses into the maturity phase and the fusion engine accumulates sufficient evidence to reduce vacuous mass, the effective threshold reverts to its standard value, and the access decision is re-evaluated against the now-richer evidence base. This temporal threshold modulation prevents the unintuitive outcome of an entity being denied access immediately after a strong authentication event simply because the fusion engine has not yet had time to accumulate corroborating behavioural evidence, while still ensuring that the initial relaxation is time-bounded and automatically reverts as the session matures (Alsubhi et al., 2024).

The practical enforcement of threshold-based access decisions requires tight architectural integration between the trust computation engine (which produces the continuous trust score), the Policy Decision Point (PDP, which evaluates the score against the applicable threshold), and the Policy Enforcement Point (PEP, which executes the resulting access action). In the proposed architecture, this integration is achieved through an Open Policy Agent (OPA) policy engine that consumes the trust score as a real-time input, evaluates it against the dynamically configured threshold matrix (which specifies per-resource, per-threat-level thresholds with hysteresis margins), and emits an access decision (full, constrained, or deny) to the Envoy proxy serving as the PEP (Mandal et al., 2023). The entire evaluation cycle — from trust score computation through policy evaluation to enforcement action — must execute within latencies that are imperceptible to the end user (typically single-digit milliseconds), ensuring that the sophistication of the trust evaluation does not come at the cost of operational responsiveness.

In synthesis, trust thresholds transform the continuous trust score produced by the temporal decay and evidence fusion mechanisms into actionable, enforceable access decisions. The graduated, three-tier access architecture replaces binary access logic with risk-proportionate decision-making that accommodates the epistemic uncertainty inherent in heterogeneous, adversarial environments. Dynamic threshold calibration ensures that the definition of "sufficient trust" evolves with the threat landscape. Hysteresis mechanisms prevent oscillatory instability. Resource-differentiated thresholds encode organisational risk appetite. And temporal threshold modulation bridges the initialisation phase where evidence is sparse but authentication is fresh. Together with the temporal decay mechanisms described in the preceding sections, trust thresholds complete the architectural pipeline from raw evidence observation through temporal discounting, evidential fusion, and threshold evaluation to the ultimate output of the continuous verification system: a dynamic, proportionate, continuously re-evaluated access decision.
