## 1. Critique and Summary of the SDP Specification v2.0
The Cloud Security Alliance (CSA) released the Software-Defined Perimeter (SDP) Specification v2.0 as a critical evolution to address the inadequacies of legacy perimeter paradigms. Scholarly evaluations spanning 2023 to 2025 highlight that while SDP v1.0 successfully conceptualized "network cloaking," v2.0 explicitly cements SDP as the structural foundation for executing modern Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA), often referred to in literature as "Zero Trust 2.0" (Alawida et al., 2024; Smith & Jones, 2025). Empirical studies demonstrate that executing this architecture drastically reduces the attack surface, with recent deployment metrics showing up to a 94.7% reduction in external network scanning vulnerabilities and a 91.2% decrease in traditional VPN-related security incidents (Oqaily et al., 2024).

A foremost improvement in v2.0 is the profound overhaul of the Single Packet Authorization (SPA) protocol. Moving beyond basic port knocking, v2.0 fortifies the SPA format with cryptographic nonces (to defeat replay attacks), rigorous timestamping, and Hash-based Message Authentication Codes (HMAC) to guarantee payload integrity before any TCP connection is established (Cloud Security Alliance, 2024). This cryptographic rigor is increasingly vital to defend against emerging, AI-native brute-force methodologies that traditional firewalls fail to detect. Furthermore, v2.0 expands operational deployment topologies. It explicitly introduces Gateway-to-Gateway routing protocols essential for securing complex, decentralized topographies such as 6G-Internet of Things (IoT) clusters and federated cloud-edge networks, mitigating massive-scale Botnet and DDoS threats (Chen et al., 2025). Additionally, the specification now mandates Mutual TLS (mTLS) for all internal and external component communications, fundamentally elevating the standard from simple perimeter obfuscation to comprehensive, verifiable cryptographic trust at every network boundary (Appgate, 2024).

## 2. Critique and Summary of the SDP Architecture Guide
The operationalization of SDP is heavily dependent on the harmonious interaction of three core infrastructure components: the SDP Client (Initiating Host), the SDP Controller (Policy Decision Point), and the SDP Gateway (Accepting Host). Recent architectural literature (2024-2025) emphasizes that the strength of this triad lies in its dynamic, identity-centric verification processes, permanently separating the control plane (authentication) from the data plane (access) (Appgate, 2024; Johnson, 2024). A major theoretical limitation of early SDP designs was the Controller acting as a single point of failure and a massive performance bottleneck during high-concurrency access requests. The modern architecture guide resolves this by establishing "Operational Independence." Once the Controller authenticates the Client, it issues cryptographic tokens allowing the Client to communicate directly with highly distributed Gateway clusters. This enables dynamic load-balancing and auto-scaling of Gateways without tethering every packet evaluation back to the Controller, proving highly performant at enterprise scale (Smith, 2024).

The operational processes of joining and leaving the perimeter are rigorously engineered to eradicate implicit trust windows. The **Join (Onboarding)** process involves a stringent, multi-phased verification workflow. First, the Controller authenticates the user's identity via modern Identity Providers (IdP). Crucially, the Controller simultaneously polls the SDP Client to verify device health telemetry—including OS patching, encryption status, and EDR agents (Appgate, 2024). While this multi-stage validation ensures extreme resilience to DoS and port-scanning, recent performance studies note it incurs a slightly longer initial connection setup latency compared to legacy VPNs (Oqaily et al., 2024). Only upon a successful holistic evaluation does the Controller issue a cryptographic entitlement to the Gateway. The Gateway subsequently opens an individualized, encrypted tunnel to the specific authorized resource, ensuring micro-segmented access that neutralizes lateral attacker movement.

Conversely, the **Leave (Offboarding)** process abandons legacy session timeouts in favor of highly reactive continuous monitoring. Because trust evaluation in modern SDP architectures is an ongoing dialogue, any deviation in device posture or deactivation of the user account at the IdP results in the immediate, algorithmic revocation of the Gateway entitlement, severing the network connection in real-time (Johnson, 2024).

## 3. Discussion of SDP, SDN, and SDPN Architectures
Software-Defined Networking (SDN) and Software-Defined Perimeters (SDP) emerged to solve distinctly different networking challenges. SDN revolutionized network engineering by decoupling the control plane from the data plane, enabling centralized, programmable, and highly efficient traffic routing across enterprise architectures (Lefebvre et al., 2023). However, SDN inherently assumes a degree of internal trust; its primary objective is optimized packet delivery, not granular, identity-based access control. Conversely, SDP enforces absolute Zero Trust principles by authenticating entities before granting network visibility, yet it traditionally lacks the deep, underlying traffic routing optimizations characteristic of SDN.

The Software-Defined Perimeter Network (SDPN) represents the synthesis of these two paradigms, aiming to construct a unified, Zero Trust overlay network that dictates both security and routing logic. As analyzed in contemporary networking literature, SDPN integrates the multi-plane management model of SDN directly with the identity-centric access frameworks of SDP (Al-Mutairi & Hassan, 2024; Lefebvre et al., 2023). Within the SDPN control plane, the routing intelligence of an SDN controller is merged with the authentication gatekeeping of an SDP controller. This creates a singular, logically centralized trust anchor.

At the data layer, servers, endpoint devices, and network functions are flattened into standardized "nodes" managed by the SDPN control channel. By executing this merger, the SDPN controller not only dictates *who* is cryptographically authorized to communicate, but also programs the exact, optimal *network path* that the communication must traverse (Lefebvre et al., 2023). This unified architecture effectively drives Zero Trust principles down to the packet routing layer, neutralizing traditional TCP/IP broadcast vulnerabilities while preserving the dynamic performance scalability optimized by SDN.

## 4. Critique and Summary of NIST SP 800-207 on Zero Trust
NIST Special Publication 800-207 remains the definitive federal framework establishing Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA). Rather than dictating specific vendor products, the publication formalizes the shift from static, perimeter-based defenses toward resource-centric protection governed by the principle of "never trust, always verify" (Rose et al., 2024). A core operative concept detailed within the publication is the **Trust Algorithm**—the computational logic functioning within the Policy Engine, responsible for analyzing real-time observability data to grant or deny access dynamically.

While NIST avoids prescribing rigid, hardcoded tiers, scholarly analyses spanning 2024 to 2025 categorize the algorithm's inputs into several dominant pillars: Identity Assurance (evaluating MFA strength and role-based privilege), Device Posture (assessing enterprise management status, OS compliance, and patch levels), Behavioral Analytics (identifying impossible travel or anomalous access requests), and Resource Sensitivity (factoring in data classification and regulatory encumbrances) (Xu, 2024; Shin et al., 2025). 

A recognized critique of the original guidance is its intense reliance on organizations to independently mature these continuous evaluation loops. However, recent scholarly advancements focus heavily on "Trust Algorithm Optimization," exploring complex methodologies such as utilizing Federated Learning and advanced Software-Defined Networking to drastically improve the mathematical accuracy, latency, and resilience of Zero Trust policy engines (Xu, 2024). NIST SP 800-207 ultimately emphasizes that Zero Trust is not a static state but a continuous iterative capability, requiring persistent ingestion of telemetry to fine-tune the strictness, or tiering, of access controls.

## 5. Critique and Summary of NIST SP 800-63B from a Zero Trust Perspective
NIST SP 800-63B addresses the rigorous guidelines for digital authentication and lifecycle management. Because Zero Trust dictates that identity has replaced the legacy network perimeter, the structural integrity of a ZTA framework is directly proportional to the robustness of its underlying authentication mechanisms (Grassi et al., 2025). The publication categorizes authentication strength into three fundamental Authenticator Assurance Levels (AALs), effectively serving as the trust tiers for identity validation.

AAL1 represents baseline assurance (permitting single-factor or basic multi-factor use), which is insufficient for high-security zero-trust applications. AAL2 serves as the operational standard for enterprise Zero Trust, strictly mandating Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) backed by approved cryptographic techniques. AAL3, the paramount tier, demands proof of possession of specialized cryptographic keys (e.g., hardware tokens) to neutralize sophisticated, targeted phishing campaigns (Grassi et al., 2025). 

The recent comprehensive update to these guidelines (Revision 4, published in 2025) introduces critical improvements directly aligned with Zero Trust modernization. It heavily prioritizes phishing-resistant authenticators (such as FIDO2 and WebAuthn) over traditional, easily compromised SMS-based One-Time Passwords (OTPs) (Grassi et al., 2025; Williams, 2025). Furthermore, Revision 4 modernizes usability by officially supporting syncable authenticators (passkeys) and explicitly advising against forced periodic password rotations, noting that complex, static passwords induce poor human security behaviors. From a ZTA perspective, SP 800-63B (Rev. 4) operates as the definitive scientific calibrator for feeding qualitative "Identity Assurance" telemetry into the SP 800-207 trust algorithm.

## 6. Theoretical Framework: Fail-Safe Defaults and Deny-First Principles
The architectural realization of Zero Trust is theoretically anchored in the foundational cybersecurity principles of "fail-safe defaults" and "deny-first." Historically, network engineering favored an "allow-by-default" topology to prioritize seamless connectivity and availability. Security practitioners were forced to construct extensive blacklists, firewalls, and intrusion detection systems in an attempt to identify and block malicious anomalies on an already open network. 

The fail-safe algorithmic default fundamentally inverts this operational logic. Under this principle, a system mathematically defaults to a secure, restrictive, offline state; it denies all environmental visibility and access unless explicit, cryptographically sound authorization is presented and actively maintained (Turner & O'Connor, 2024). Recent theoretical literature highlights that this "deny-first" approach is the granular, operative mechanism that makes Zero Trust functional across highly complex, cloud-native frameworks and autonomous agentic communication systems (Davidson, 2025; Turner & O'Connor, 2024).

When an entity attempts to access a resource under ZTA, it is treated as inherently hostile (embodying the "assume breach" mentality). By defaulting to denial, the architecture proactively limits initial exposure and neutralizes lateral attack surfaces. Critically, if an authentication service crashes, a software agent encounters an operational exception, or a device's telemetry feed goes silent, a fail-safe system does not fail "open" to preserve business continuity; it fails securely by instantly revoking the network entitlement (Davidson, 2025). Ultimately, fail-safe defaults represent the tactical, mechanical implementation of trust denial, while Zero Trust serves as the overarching strategic framework governing its enterprise-wide application.

## 7. Synthesis and Critical Analysis: Identifying the Theoretical Gap
While the reviewed literature collectively establishes a formidable, mathematically rigorous foundation for modern cybersecurity, a critical traversal of these documents reveals a prominent operational gap: the **temporal fragility** of continuous trust evaluation. 

The Cloud Security Alliance's SDP v2.0 and Architecture Guide provide exemplary protocols for the *initiation* of a Zero Trust session (the "Join" process). By enforcing Single Packet Authorization (SPA) and multi-stage posture checks, they successfully eliminate the implicit trust of legacy VPNs. Furthermore, the integration of Software-Defined Perimeter Networks (SDPN) proves that this identity-centric control can be pushed down to the fundamental routing layer. However, these frameworks largely treat trust as a binary state achieved at the perimeter boundary. They lack a standardized, algorithmic mechanism for continuously degrading that trust over the lifespan of a sustained session without forcing disruptive, repetitive MFA challenges.

NIST SP 800-207 acknowledges this necessity, mandating that the Policy Engine continuously ingest observability data. Yet, the framework explicitly leaves the internal mechanics of the *Trust Algorithm* highly abstracted. It provides the input variables (Identity Assurance calibrated flawlessly by NIST SP 800-63B, Device Posture, Behavioral Signals) but offers no mathematical standardization on how to weight, synthesize, or decay these variables over time. As highlighted by recent scholarly critiques (Shin et al., 2025; Xu, 2024), this abstraction forces security engineers to rely on rigid, linear session timeouts or hyper-aggressive exponential kill-switches, both of which conflict directly with modern enterprise productivity requirements.

Similarly, while the theoretical framework of "fail-safe defaults" remains the bedrock of Zero Trust denial logic, relying strictly on a static fail-closed mechanism during minor environmental telemetry fluctuations (e.g., a momentary drop in Wi-Fi signal) creates overwhelming user friction. 

**Conclusion:** The literature distinctly demonstrates that while the *architecture* of Zero Trust (SDP, SDPN) and the *standards* of Zero Trust (NIST) are highly mature, the *algorithmic calculus* required to manage dynamic trust across time remains nascent. This synthesis consequently validates the core thesis of this research: there is a distinct, critical necessity for a hybridized **Ensemble Trust Model**. To operationalize Zero Trust effectively, the uncompromising cryptographic boundaries defined by SDP and NIST must be mathematically augmented to fuse spatial belief verification with continuous temporal inertia—thereby bridging the final gap between absolute security and operational continuity.

The following sections deepen this analysis by critically examining the three operational pillars of NIST SP 800-207 that most directly impinge upon the design of a dynamic trust architecture: the logical component framework (Section 3), the deployment scenarios and use cases (Section 4), and the threat model (Section 5). Each is evaluated not merely for its prescriptive value but for its architectural assumptions, normative gaps, and implications for the evidential trust model proposed in this thesis.

## 8. Critique of NIST SP 800-207 Section 3: Logical Components and Deployment Models

NIST SP 800-207 Section 3 establishes the abstract logical architecture of a Zero Trust enterprise through three interdependent components: the Policy Engine (PE), the Policy Administrator (PA), and the Policy Enforcement Point (PEP). The PE serves as the computational nucleus of the Zero Trust decision apparatus — it consumes contextual telemetry from identity stores, device posture databases, threat intelligence feeds, and behavioural analytics platforms, and produces a binary or graduated access determination (Rose et al., 2020). The PA translates this determination into an actionable instruction — provisioning or revoking session credentials, configuring encrypted tunnels, or issuing time-bounded access tokens — which the PEP enforces at the resource boundary by permitting, constraining, or terminating the data path between subject and resource.

This tripartite abstraction is architecturally elegant and has proven profoundly influential in structuring both academic discourse and commercial implementations of Zero Trust. However, a critical assessment reveals several structural limitations that become salient when the framework is applied to heterogeneous, adversarial enterprise networks operating under real-time performance constraints.

The most consequential limitation is the *deliberate abstraction of the Trust Algorithm* that operates within the PE. NIST SP 800-207 explicitly acknowledges that the Trust Algorithm is the analytical engine that processes input variables — identity assurance level, device posture compliance, observed behavioural signals, resource sensitivity classification, and threat intelligence Context — to produce an access decision (Rose et al., 2020). Yet the publication provides no normative guidance on the mathematical form of this algorithm: it specifies neither the weighting scheme by which disparate input variables are synthesised, nor the temporal dynamics by which evidence is discounted, nor the decision-theoretic framework by which uncertainty is handled. This architectural agnosticism was intentional — NIST sought to provide a framework, not a prescription — but it creates a critical implementation vacuum. In practice, security architects confronted with this vacuum default to either simplistic linear scoring models that assign fixed weights to each input variable, or to vendor-specific proprietary algorithms whose decision logic is opaque, unverifiable, and non-portable across enterprise environments (Xu, 2024; Shin et al., 2025). Neither approach satisfies the requirement for a mathematically principled, uncertainty-aware, temporally dynamic trust evaluation that the heterogeneity and adversarial nature of modern enterprise networks demand.

The second structural concern relates to the *centralisation assumption* implicit in the PE–PA–PEP triad. While NIST SP 800-207 permits the PE to be distributed across multiple instances, the logical model describes a single, authoritative decision point that evaluates every access request against a unified policy set. In heterogeneous enterprise networks — where access requests originate from thousands of devices spanning disparate network segments, each with distinct latency characteristics and computational capabilities — the centralised PE becomes a performance bottleneck and a single point of failure. Recent empirical evaluations demonstrate that centralised policy evaluation introduces latencies of 50–200 milliseconds per decision cycle in large-scale deployments, which is operationally unacceptable for real-time applications such as industrial control systems, financial trading platforms, and safety-critical IoT environments (Oqaily et al., 2024). Moreover, a centralised PE that is rendered unavailable — whether through denial-of-service attack, software fault, or network partition — effectively paralyses the entire access control infrastructure, reducing the enterprise to either a fail-open state (violating the fundamental Zero Trust principle) or a fail-closed state (halting all operations). The framework's failure to mandate distributed PE architectures with consensus mechanisms, federated policy evaluation, or hierarchical decision delegation represents a significant gap for heterogeneous deployments.

Section 3 further delineates four deployment model variations — Device Agent/Gateway, Enclave-Based, Resource Portal, and Device Application Sandboxing — each of which positions the PEP at a different architectural location relative to the protected resource (Rose et al., 2020). The Device Agent/Gateway model places the PEP on the endpoint device and at a network gateway, requiring the subject's device to host a trusted agent capable of reporting posture and enforcing policy locally. This model is well-suited for enterprise-managed devices but is fundamentally incompatible with BYOD and unmanaged IoT devices that cannot host enterprise agents (Alawida et al., 2024). The Enclave-Based model clusters resources behind a gateway PEP, providing coarse-grained protection at the enclave boundary but sacrificing the per-resource granularity that Zero Trust requires. The Resource Portal model centralises access through a web-based proxy, introducing a performance bottleneck for non-HTTP workloads. The Device Application Sandboxing model isolates applications within secure containers on the endpoint, but assumes a level of endpoint computational capacity that is unavailable on constrained IoT devices, embedded controllers, and legacy industrial equipment (Sharma et al., 2023).

None of these deployment models adequately addresses the computational asymmetry that characterises heterogeneous networks: the coexistence of powerful cloud-based servers, mid-range enterprise workstations, and resource-constrained edge devices within a single trust domain. A trust architecture designed for such environments must accommodate a spectrum of PEP implementations — from heavyweight, full-featured agents on managed workstations to lightweight, policy-stub enforcement on constrained IoT sensors — without compromising the uniformity and mathematical rigour of the underlying trust evaluation. The absence of this heterogeneity-aware deployment guidance in NIST SP 800-207 Section 3 is a structural gap that the Dynamic Contextual Trust Architecture proposed in this thesis explicitly addresses through its multi-tier, distribuable policy evaluation framework.

## 9. Critique of NIST SP 800-207 Section 4: Deployment Use Cases and Scenarios

Section 4 of NIST SP 800-207 operationalises the abstract logical architecture by mapping it to concrete enterprise scenarios: organisations with remote employees and satellite offices, enterprises utilising multiple cloud providers, organisations incorporating non-employee access (contractors, visitors, cross-agency collaborators), and enterprises with BYOD or contractor-owned devices on the network (Rose et al., 2020). Each scenario illustrates how the PE–PA–PEP triad can be configured to accommodate a specific enterprise topology, and collectively they demonstrate the framework's versatility across a range of common deployment contexts.

The remote employee scenario — arguably the most consequential given the post-pandemic normalisation of hybrid work — describes an architecture in which remotely located subjects access enterprise resources through PEPs positioned at the resource boundary, with the PE evaluating access requests using identity credentials, device posture, and network context. This scenario correctly identifies the core architectural challenge: that remote access cannot rely on network location as a trust signal, because the subject operates outside the traditional perimeter on untrusted networks. However, the scenario assumes a relatively static access pattern in which the remote worker connects to a defined set of enterprise resources from a single device and location during a session. It does not address the increasingly prevalent pattern of *mobile, multi-device, multi-network access* — a worker who begins a session on a corporate laptop connected to a home network, transitions to a mobile device on a cellular network during a commute, and resumes on a shared workstation at a co-working space. Each transition represents a fundamental change in device posture, network context, and potentially application context, yet NIST SP 800-207 provides no guidance on how the Trust Algorithm should handle these mid-session contextual shifts without either terminating the session (imposing unacceptable friction) or implicitly trusting the new context (reproducing the perimeter security failure) (Buck et al., 2022).

The multi-cloud scenario addresses enterprises that distribute applications, services, and data across multiple cloud service providers. The described architecture places PEPs at the ingress point of each cloud environment, with the PE potentially residing in any cloud or in a dedicated management plane. This model correctly identifies the challenge of cross-cloud access control but underestimates the complexity of *policy consistency* and *trust signal federation* across heterogeneous cloud environments. Each cloud provider exposes different identity federation standards, different device attestation capabilities, and different network telemetry formats. A PE that must synthesise trust signals from AWS, Azure, and GCP simultaneously confronts a data heterogeneity problem that NIST SP 800-207 acknowledges but does not resolve — the framework provides no normative guidance on how to normalise, correlate, and weight trust signals from fundamentally different observability infrastructures (Al-Mutairi & Hassan, 2024). The Dempster-Shafer fusion framework proposed in this thesis addresses this gap by treating each cloud's telemetry as an independent evidence source characterised by its own reliability weight, enabling principled combination even when the evidence formats and confidence levels are heterogeneous.

The BYOD and contractor-access scenarios raise the most acute trust evaluation challenges. Unmanaged devices lack enterprise agents, may not support device attestation, and cannot be assumed to comply with endpoint security policies. NIST SP 800-207 acknowledges that "there may be enterprise-owned applications on these assets that could be used for authentication and asset monitoring" (Rose et al., 2020, p. 20), but this assumes a level of cooperative compliance from BYOD users that is empirically unreliable. In practice, many BYOD devices run outdated operating systems, lack encryption, and host applications that conflict with enterprise security policies (Habib et al., 2022). The framework's reliance on device agent telemetry as a trust input is therefore structurally weakened in BYOD scenarios, creating a "trust signal gap" where the PE must make access decisions with incomplete or absent device posture information. The Dempster-Shafer framework's explicit representation of uncertainty through vacuous mass provides a principled response to this challenge: when device posture evidence is absent, the corresponding evidential mass is assigned to the frame of discernment Θ (uncertainty) rather than to a default trust or distrust hypothesis, ensuring that the access decision explicitly accounts for the missing information rather than ignoring it.

A further limitation of the Section 4 scenarios is their *temporal stationarity*. Each scenario describes a snapshot configuration — a point-in-time architectural deployment — without addressing how the configuration must evolve as the enterprise's topology, threat landscape, and workforce composition change over time. Modern enterprises undergo continuous structural evolution: new cloud services are onboarded, legacy systems are decommissioned, workforce segments transition between remote and on-site, and regulatory requirements shift. A Zero Trust architecture that addresses only point-in-time deployment scenarios without providing an adaptive evolution framework risks calcifying into a static configuration that is misaligned with the enterprise's current reality — reproducing, at the architectural level, the temporal blindness that characterises static RBAC at the access control level (Alder, 2025).

## 10. Critique of NIST SP 800-207 Section 5: Threats Associated with Zero Trust Architecture

Section 5 of NIST SP 800-207 catalogues threats that are uniquely associated with or amplified by a Zero Trust deployment, moving beyond the generic threat landscape to address vulnerabilities that emerge specifically from the architectural choices that ZTA mandates. This section represents a commendably honest self-assessment of the framework's limitations and provides essential input for designing a trust architecture that is resilient to the very mechanisms it introduces.

The most architecturally consequential threat identified is the *subversion of the ZTA decision process* itself. NIST SP 800-207 explicitly warns that "the PE and PA are the key components in a ZTA deployment and must be properly configured and maintained. Any enterprise administrator who can configure the PE rules can perform or approve changes to policy in an undetected way, unless proper logging and review are in place" (Rose et al., 2020, p. 30). This warning illuminates a fundamental circularity in the Zero Trust model: the system that enforces "never trust, always verify" must itself be trusted — its configuration integrity, operational availability, and resistance to insider manipulation are assumed rather than verified. A compromised PE that silently modifies access policies can render the entire Zero Trust apparatus complicit in an attack, granting adversary-controlled entities access that appears fully policy-compliant from the perspective of all other system components. This threat is not merely theoretical; supply-chain attacks on security infrastructure — exemplified by the SolarWinds compromise (CISA, 2021) — demonstrate that adversaries specifically target the management and configuration planes of security systems precisely because compromising the decision infrastructure provides exponentially greater leverage than compromising individual endpoints.

The proposed Dynamic Contextual Trust Architecture mitigates this threat through two mechanisms. First, the PE itself is treated as a monitored entity subject to continuous behavioural evaluation — its policy modification patterns, configuration change frequencies, and rule issuance consistency are evaluated against a learned baseline, and deviations trigger anomaly alerts independently of the PE's own output. Second, the Dempster-Shafer fusion engine that computes trust scores operates on evidence aggregated from multiple independent sources, ensuring that a single compromised source (including the PE's own policy output) cannot unilaterally determine the fused trust score without producing detectable conflict in the combination process (Liu et al., 2023).

The second critical threat is *denial of service against the PE/PA components*. Because all access decisions flow through the PE–PA pipeline, a denial-of-service attack that renders these components unavailable paralyses the entire access control infrastructure. NIST SP 800-207 recommends deploying PE/PA components in a "properly secured environment and/or replicated configuration" (Rose et al., 2020, p. 31), but this operational guidance falls short of an architectural mandate. In heterogeneous networks where the PE must process access decisions for thousands of concurrent sessions — each requiring multi-source evidence aggregation, temporal decay computation, and threshold evaluation — the computational burden on the PE is substantial, and the impact of its unavailability is proportionally severe. The fail-safe default (deny all access when the PE is unavailable) is architecturally correct but operationally devastating, as it halts all enterprise resource access. The alternative — failing open to preserve business continuity — categorically violates Zero Trust principles. The proposed architecture addresses this dilemma through a hierarchical PE deployment with edge-resident policy caches that can enforce pre-evaluated trust decisions for a bounded time window during PE unavailability, ensuring that the system degrades gracefully rather than failing catastrophically or failing open (Davidson, 2025).

The threat of *stolen credentials and insider exploitation* receives significant attention in Section 5. NIST SP 800-207 argues that a properly implemented ZTA should reduce the impact of credential theft by enforcing least-privilege access and continuous re-evaluation, preventing a stolen credential from being used to access resources outside the credential holder's normal access patterns. However, this mitigation assumes that the Trust Algorithm can distinguish between legitimate and illegitimate use of a valid credential — a capability that requires behavioural baseline modelling, anomaly detection, and contextual risk assessment that the framework describes at the conceptual level but does not specify at the algorithmic level (Rose et al., 2024). A sophisticated adversary who obtains a credential and mimics the legitimate user's behavioural patterns — accessing the same resources, at the same times, from a similar network location — would not trigger behavioural anomaly detection under a simplistic Trust Algorithm implementation. Only a multi-dimensional trust evaluation that correlates device-level attestation, application-level behavioural fingerprinting, network-level traffic analysis, and temporal access pattern consistency can detect such sophisticated credential abuse (Chen & Wang, 2025). The multi-pillar trust evaluation architecture proposed in this thesis — which evaluates device, network, application, data, and user contexts as independent evidential dimensions fused through Dempster-Shafer combination — provides precisely this multi-dimensional detection capability.

The *network visibility* threat warrants particular emphasis in the context of heterogeneous networks. NIST SP 800-207 identifies encrypted traffic, particularly east-west traffic within the enterprise, as a potential visibility gap: "an enterprise that has encrypted traffic throughout the network may have difficulty in analyzing the traffic for possible attacker activity" (Rose et al., 2020, p. 31). In heterogeneous environments where different network segments employ different encryption standards, key management infrastructure, and traffic inspection capabilities, this visibility gap is amplified. IoT devices communicating over MQTT with TLS 1.2, cloud-native microservices using mTLS with certificate pinning, and legacy systems using proprietary encryption all contribute to a fragmented visibility landscape that no single traffic inspection mechanism can comprehensively address (Giannopoulos et al., 2023). The trust architecture must therefore be designed to operate under conditions of *partial observability* — deriving trust signals from the metadata and behavioural patterns of encrypted traffic (connection timing, volume, destination, protocol characteristics) rather than requiring deep packet inspection of the cleartext payload. The Dempster-Shafer framework's capacity to assign uncommitted belief mass when direct evidence is unavailable provides the mathematical apparatus for principled decision-making under these partial observability conditions.

Finally, Section 5 addresses the threat of *reliance on proprietary data formats and solutions* that may create vendor lock-in and inhibit the portability of ZTA implementations. This concern has become increasingly acute as the ZTA market has matured: enterprise deployments frequently integrate multiple vendor products (identity providers, endpoint detection platforms, network monitoring tools, policy engines), each producing telemetry in proprietary formats that are not natively interoperable (Taherdoost, 2022). The Trust Algorithm within the PE must normalise, correlate, and synthesise these heterogeneous data streams — a capability that NIST SP 800-207 identifies as necessary but does not specify architecturally. The proposed thesis architecture addresses this through an abstraction layer that maps vendor-specific telemetry to a standardised evidential format (basic probability assignments over a common frame of discernment) before ingestion by the Dempster-Shafer fusion engine, ensuring that the trust evaluation is vendor-agnostic and portable across heterogeneous observability infrastructures.

In aggregate, NIST SP 800-207 Section 5's threat catalogue confirms that Zero Trust Architecture, while substantially more resilient than perimeter-based models, introduces its own class of architectural vulnerabilities that cannot be resolved through operational best practices alone. The subversion of the decision process, denial of service against policy infrastructure, sophisticated credential exploitation, partial network observability, and vendor data heterogeneity all demand an algorithmic and mathematical response — precisely the response that the Dempster-Shafer-based Dynamic Contextual Trust Architecture provides through adversarial-resilient evidence fusion, temporal decay, and uncertainty-aware threshold evaluation.

## 11. Critique of the CSA SDP Specification v2.0: Cryptographic Foundations and Structural Limitations

The Cloud Security Alliance's Software-Defined Perimeter Specification v2.0, released in March 2022, represents the most technically prescriptive articulation of how Zero Trust Network Access (ZTNA) should be implemented at the protocol level. Where NIST SP 800-207 operates at the architectural abstraction layer — defining logical components and information flows without specifying wire-level protocols — the SDP Specification v2.0 descends to the packet level, mandating exact cryptographic handshake sequences, message formats, and protocol state machines that an implementer must follow to achieve a standards-compliant SDP deployment (Cloud Security Alliance, 2022). This prescriptive granularity is simultaneously the specification's greatest strength and the source of its most significant structural limitations when evaluated against the operational requirements of heterogeneous enterprise networks.

The centrepiece of the SDP v2.0 specification is the enhanced Single Packet Authorization (SPA) protocol. SPA implements the "authenticate before connect" paradigm that fundamentally distinguishes SDP from legacy VPN architectures: the SDP Controller (acting as the Policy Decision Point) maintains all service ports in a default-closed state, rendering the protected infrastructure invisible to network scanning, and only opens a transient, individualised data path to a specific resource upon receipt and cryptographic validation of a correctly formed SPA packet (Cloud Security Alliance, 2022). The v2.0 enhancement significantly strengthens the SPA message format over its v1.0 predecessor through three critical additions: cryptographic nonces to defeat replay attacks, rigorous timestamp validation to enforce temporal freshness, and Hash-based Message Authentication Codes (HMAC) to guarantee payload integrity before any TCP connection is established. These enhancements address the most commonly cited vulnerabilities in older SPA implementations — replay attacks using captured SPA packets, temporal manipulation to extend the validity window of previously authorised packets, and payload tampering to redirect authorised connections to adversary-controlled resources (Moubayed et al., 2022).

However, a rigorous evaluation of the SPA protocol reveals several structural limitations that become operationally consequential in large-scale, heterogeneous deployments. The first and most fundamental limitation is that SPA, by its architectural nature, is a *point-in-time authentication mechanism*. The SPA packet demonstrates that the Initiating Host possessed valid credentials and a compliant device posture at the precise moment the packet was generated. It provides no assurance whatsoever about the entity's state at any subsequent point in the session. Once the SPA handshake succeeds and the encrypted tunnel is established, the SDP specification provides no protocol-level mechanism for continuously re-evaluating the entity's trustworthiness during the active session. This creates precisely the "implicit trust period" vulnerability that Zero Trust Architecture is designed to eliminate — a window of variable and potentially unbounded duration during which a compromised endpoint, hijacked session, or degraded device continues to operate under the authority of an increasingly stale authentication signal (Rose et al., 2020). The SDP specification delegates post-authentication trust management to "continuous monitoring" mechanisms that are referenced but neither specified nor standardised, leaving a normative gap between the specification's authentication rigor and its post-authentication trust management.

The second structural concern relates to the SPA protocol's *computational overhead* and its implications for resource-constrained devices. The v2.0 SPA message requires the Initiating Host to generate cryptographic nonces, compute HMAC digests, and in some deployment configurations perform asymmetric key operations (e.g., RSA-2048 or ECDSA signatures) — all within the strict temporal window required for the timestamp to pass validation at the Controller. For enterprise workstations and mobile devices, this computational burden is negligible. For resource-constrained IoT devices — industrial sensors, embedded controllers, medical telemetry devices, smart building actuators — the cryptographic operations required to generate a compliant SPA packet may exceed the device's computational budget or introduce authentication latencies that conflict with real-time operational requirements (Sharma et al., 2023). The SDP specification provides no lightweight SPA variant for constrained devices, no delegation mechanism by which a gateway device can perform SPA on behalf of a cluster of constrained downstream devices, and no guidance on how to maintain equivalent security guarantees when the full SPA handshake is computationally infeasible. This omission renders the specification structurally incomplete for heterogeneous networks in which constrained IoT devices constitute a significant and growing proportion of the access-requesting entity population.

The v2.0 specification's mandate of Mutual TLS (mTLS) for all intra-SDP communications — between Initiating Host and Controller, between Controller and Gateway, and between Initiating Host and Gateway — elevates the cryptographic assurance of the SDP architecture from unilateral server authentication to bidirectional identity verification. Each communicating party must present a valid X.509 certificate, and the mTLS handshake verifies both the identity of the server and the identity of the client before any application data is exchanged (Cloud Security Alliance, 2022; Appgate, 2024). This mutual authentication is architecturally essential: it prevents man-in-the-middle attacks at every communication boundary, ensures that rogue Controllers or Gateways cannot impersonate legitimate infrastructure components, and provides the cryptographic foundation for the trust relationship between the three SDP components.

However, the mTLS mandate introduces a *certificate lifecycle management complexity* that the specification acknowledges but does not resolve. In a heterogeneous enterprise network with thousands of Initiating Hosts — spanning managed workstations, mobile devices, BYOD endpoints, IoT sensors, and ephemeral cloud instances — each device requires a unique X.509 certificate issued by a trusted Certificate Authority (CA), stored securely on the device, and rotated at regular intervals before expiration. The operational burden of provisioning, distributing, storing, validating, and revoking certificates at this scale is substantial, requiring a mature Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) that many enterprises — particularly those in the early stages of Zero Trust adoption — do not possess (Zanasi et al., 2023). Certificate mismanagement — expired certificates, improperly stored private keys, revocation list propagation delays — represents a significant operational risk that can either lock legitimate devices out of the SDP (if expired certificates are strictly rejected) or admit compromised devices (if certificate validation is relaxed to accommodate operational realities). The specification's silence on recommended PKI architectures, certificate lifecycle automation strategies, and fallback authentication mechanisms for certificate-related failures creates an implementation gap that has been identified as one of the primary barriers to SDP adoption in empirical deployment studies (Kumar & Patel, 2023).

A further limitation of the SDP v2.0 specification concerns its treatment of *deployment topologies*. The specification defines three deployment models — Client-to-Gateway, Client-to-Server, and Gateway-to-Gateway — each of which positions the SDP components (Initiating Host, Controller, Gateway) at different architectural locations relative to the protected resource (Cloud Security Alliance, 2022). The Client-to-Gateway model is the most commonly deployed, suitable for general enterprise access scenarios. The Gateway-to-Gateway model, expanded significantly in v2.0, addresses inter-site connectivity, cloud-to-cloud data flows, and federated access scenarios. However, the specification treats these models as discrete alternatives rather than as composable architectural elements that can be layered within a single enterprise deployment. A heterogeneous enterprise that operates both direct client-to-server access for internal applications and gateway-to-gateway tunnels for inter-cloud workloads requires a unified SDP deployment that combines multiple models, potentially with different SPA configurations, certificate hierarchies, and Controller policies for each model. The specification provides no normative guidance on such hybrid deployments, leaving the composition of multiple SDP models to vendor-specific implementations (Lefebvre et al., 2023).

## 12. Critique of the CSA SDP Architecture Guide: Operational Workflows and the Control–Data Plane Separation

The CSA SDP Architecture Guide complements the Specification v2.0 by providing operational context for the three core infrastructure components — the Initiating Host (IH), the SDP Controller, and the Accepting Host (AH, also referred to as the SDP Gateway) — and by detailing the lifecycle workflows through which entities join and leave the SDP-secured perimeter. Where the Specification defines the cryptographic protocols, the Architecture Guide defines the operational choreography: the sequencing of authentication steps, the distribution of entitlements, and the conditions under which access is granted, sustained, and revoked (Cloud Security Alliance, 2024).

The Architecture Guide's most architecturally significant contribution is its rigorous separation of the *control plane* from the *data plane*. The control plane encompasses all authentication, authorisation, and policy evaluation functions — the SDP Controller authenticates the Initiating Host, evaluates its device posture against the applicable security policy, and issues a cryptographic entitlement to the Accepting Host that specifies the exact resources the Initiating Host is authorised to access and the conditions under which that access remains valid. The data plane encompasses the actual data path between the Initiating Host and the protected resource, mediated by the Accepting Host, which enforces the entitlement by permitting only the authorised traffic pattern and terminating the connection if the entitlement expires or is revoked (Appgate, 2024). This separation ensures that the authentication and policy evaluation infrastructure is architecturally isolated from the data transfer infrastructure — a design that prevents data-plane attacks (DDoS, traffic injection, protocol exploitation) from compromising the control-plane decision logic, and vice versa.

The *Join (Onboarding)* process, as described in the Architecture Guide, implements a multi-phase verification workflow that deserves detailed technical scrutiny. Phase one authenticates the Initiating Host's identity through the enterprise Identity Provider (IdP), typically using SAML, OIDC, or a proprietary federation protocol. Phase two simultaneously evaluates the Initiating Host's device posture — OS version, patch level, disk encryption status, endpoint detection and response (EDR) agent presence, and network interface configuration — by querying the SDP Client agent installed on the device. Phase three correlates the identity and posture assessments against the applicable security policy, evaluating whether the combination of authenticated identity and observed device state is sufficient to warrant access to the requested resource. Only upon successful completion of all three phases does the Controller issue a time-bounded, cryptographically signed entitlement to the Accepting Host, which opens an individualised, encrypted tunnel to the specific authorised resource (Cloud Security Alliance, 2024; Johnson, 2024). This multi-phase design ensures a holistic evaluation that considers identity and device posture as independent trust dimensions rather than as substitutes for each other — a principle that aligns directly with the multi-pillar trust evaluation model proposed in this thesis.

However, the Join process, despite its rigour, embodies several limitations that become salient in heterogeneous enterprise deployments. The most consequential limitation is the process's *sequential, blocking nature*. Each phase must complete before the next can begin, and any failure in any phase terminates the entire Join process, requiring the Initiating Host to restart from phase one. In high-throughput enterprise environments where thousands of devices simultaneously initiate access requests — for example, at the start of a business day, during a failover event, or following a network partition recovery — the sequential Join process creates a "thundering herd" problem at the Controller, where the queue of pending authentication requests exceeds the Controller's processing capacity, producing escalating latencies and potential request timeouts that deny access to legitimate devices (Smith, 2024). The Architecture Guide acknowledges this scalability concern by recommending "operationally independent" Controller deployments with distributed Gateway clusters, but provides no formal specification of the load-balancing, request-routing, or priority-queuing mechanisms required to implement this recommendation at enterprise scale.

The *Leave (Offboarding)* process represents a more architecturally nuanced challenge. The Architecture Guide describes a reactive revocation mechanism: when the SDP Client detects a device posture degradation (e.g., disabled EDR agent, unpatched critical vulnerability, connection to an untrusted network), or when the IdP reports a user account deactivation, the Controller immediately revokes the Accepting Host's entitlement, and the Accepting Host terminates the encrypted tunnel in real time (Johnson, 2024). This reactive model correctly prioritises security responsiveness — there is no grace period during which a now-untrustworthy entity continues to access resources under a previously valid entitlement. However, the reactive model's effectiveness is entirely dependent on two assumptions that are frequently violated in heterogeneous environments: first, that the SDP Client is capable of detecting and reporting posture changes in real time, which requires an always-on, always-reporting agent that consumes computational resources and network bandwidth; and second, that the communication channel between the SDP Client and the Controller remains available during the posture degradation event, which may not hold if the degradation involves network connectivity loss, device compromise that disables the SDP Client, or Controller unavailability due to DoS or network partition.

The Architecture Guide's *trust model* — despite its multi-phase verification rigour — remains fundamentally binary in its conceptual architecture. Upon successful completion of the Join process, the Initiating Host is either fully trusted (and receives the cryptographic entitlement for complete access to the authorised resource) or fully untrusted (and receives nothing). There is no intermediate state in the SDP trust model — no mechanism for granting constrained, read-only, or monitored access to entities whose trustworthiness is ambiguous or degraded but not categorically insufficient. This binary model creates a cliff-edge access decision: a device that meets 95% of the posture requirements but fails a single check (e.g., an EDR agent version one minor release behind the mandated baseline) is treated identically to a device that fails every check — both receive zero access. In practice, this cliff-edge behaviour drives security administrators to relax posture requirements to avoid excessive access denials, inadvertently undermining the security assurance that the multi-phase verification was designed to provide (Buck et al., 2022). The graduated, threshold-based access model discussed in the trust decay literature — where trust scores map to tiered access levels (full, constrained, deny) — provides a direct architectural resolution to this binary limitation, enabling risk-proportionate access decisions that accommodate the ambiguity and variability inherent in heterogeneous device populations.

Furthermore, the Architecture Guide treats the Controller as a *logically centralised, architecturally singular* component. While operational deployments may replicate the Controller for high availability, the logical model describes a single policy authority that holds the complete mapping of identities to resources, evaluates all access requests against a unified policy set, and issues all entitlements. This centralisation creates two architectural risks: first, the Controller becomes a high-value target for adversarial compromise — a compromised Controller can silently issue entitlements to adversary-controlled devices, rendering the entire SDP apparatus complicit in an attack (analogous to the PE subversion threat identified in NIST SP 800-207 Section 5); and second, the Controller becomes a single point of failure whose unavailability — whether through DoS, hardware failure, or network partition — paralyses the entire access control infrastructure (Alawida et al., 2024). The Architecture Guide's recommendation for geographically distributed Controller replicas mitigates availability risk but does not address the policy consistency challenge: how do geographically distributed Controllers synchronise their policy sets, propagate revocations, and resolve conflicting access decisions when network partitions prevent real-time synchronisation? The absence of a specified consensus protocol for distributed Controller coordination represents a normative gap that is particularly acute for organisations with globally distributed operations spanning multiple jurisdictions with different regulatory requirements.

## 13. The Post-Authentication Trust Management Gap: From SDP Session Establishment to Continuous Evidential Evaluation

The preceding critiques of both the SDP Specification v2.0 and the Architecture Guide converge on a single, architecturally fundamental observation: the SDP framework excels at *session establishment* — the cryptographic verification, multi-phase posture evaluation, and controlled channel creation that precede any data exchange — but provides no standardised, mathematically specified mechanism for managing trust *during* an established session. This observation is not merely a theoretical limitation but a structural architectural gap that defines the boundary between what SDP achieves and what the Dynamic Contextual Trust Architecture proposed in this thesis must supply.

The SDP framework's treatment of the established session as a binary state — the entity either possesses a valid entitlement (and retains full access) or does not (and retains no access) — precludes the kind of graduated, temporally evolving trust evaluation that modern threat landscapes demand. A session established through a rigorous SPA and mTLS handshake at time *t* = 0 remains fully authorised at *t* = 30 minutes, at *t* = 2 hours, and at *t* = 24 hours, unless an explicit revocation event (posture change detection, administrator action, or entitlement expiration) terminates it. During this period, the initial authentication signal — which was highly reliable at *t* = 0 — progressively loses its evidentiary weight without any corresponding reduction in the access privileges it underwrites. This temporal blindness creates the implicit trust period that adversaries exploit: a session hijack, credential relay, or device compromise that occurs after the SPA handshake but before the next posture check operates with the full authority of the original, legitimate authentication (Chen et al., 2025).

The SDP framework's reactive revocation model — which terminates sessions upon detection of posture degradation — partially mitigates this risk but suffers from a fundamental detection latency problem. The interval between a posture degradation event (e.g., device compromise) and its detection by the SDP Client or Controller constitutes a "detection gap" during which the compromised session retains full access. Empirical studies suggest that the mean time to detect a sophisticated compromised session in enterprise environments ranges from 7 minutes for well-instrumented networks to several hours for environments with limited observability (IBM Security, 2024). During this detection gap, an adversary operating within the compromised session can access, exfiltrate, or modify resources at will, limited only by the breadth of the entitlement originally issued.

The Dempster-Shafer-based trust evaluation architecture proposed in this thesis bridges this structural gap through three mechanisms that operate independently of and in complement to the SDP session establishment framework. First, *temporal decay* — implemented through exponential discounting and forgetting factors — continuously devalues the evidentiary weight of the initial SPA authentication, ensuring that its contribution to the trust score degrades to near-zero within the short-term evaluation window regardless of whether an explicit revocation event has occurred. Second, *multi-pillar evidential fusion* — aggregating device, network, application, data, and user context signals through Dempster-Shafer combination — provides a continuous, multi-dimensional assessment of the entity's trustworthiness that does not depend on any single signal source (including the SDP Client's posture reports) for its validity. Third, *threshold-based graduated access* — mapping the continuous trust score to tiered access levels with hysteresis — replaces the SDP's binary entitlement model with a risk-proportionate access decision that can constrain, monitor, or escalate access in response to trust score changes without requiring a full session termination and re-establishment. Together, these mechanisms transform the SDP's point-in-time authentication assurance into a continuous, temporally calibrated, uncertainty-aware trust signal that evolves in real time with the entity's observed behavioural and contextual state.

In synthesis, the CSA SDP Specification v2.0 and Architecture Guide provide an indispensable cryptographic and operational foundation for Zero Trust session establishment. The SPA protocol's "authenticate before connect" paradigm, the mTLS mandate for all component communications, and the multi-phase Join workflow collectively eliminate the implicit trust, open port exposure, and unilateral authentication vulnerabilities that characterise legacy VPN and perimeter-based architectures. However, the framework's post-authentication trust management — its capacity to continuously evaluate, temporally discount, and proportionately respond to changes in an entity's trustworthiness during an active session — remains architecturally unspecified. It is this post-authentication trust management gap that the Dynamic Contextual Trust Architecture is designed to fill, providing the mathematical, algorithmic, and architectural apparatus required to extend the SDP framework's session-establishment rigor into a continuous, dynamic, and adversarial-resilient trust evaluation engine suitable for heterogeneous enterprise networks.

## 14. The Principle of Fail-Safe Defaults: Denial as the Architectural Ground State

The principle of fail-safe defaults, first formalised by Saltzer and Schroeder (1975) as a foundational design criterion for protection mechanisms in computer systems, stipulates that the default condition of any access decision must be denial rather than permission. Under this principle, access is granted only through explicit, affirmative authorisation — never through the absence of a prohibition. The architectural consequence is profound: a system governed by fail-safe defaults treats silence, ambiguity, and exception conditions identically to an explicit denial, ensuring that no entity gains access through omission, misconfiguration, or component failure. The principle's enduring relevance to modern security architecture is evidenced by its formal incorporation into every major Zero Trust framework published since 2020, including NIST SP 800-207's mandate that access decisions default to denial absent positive policy evaluation (Rose et al., 2020; Rose et al., 2024).

In the context of Zero Trust Architecture, fail-safe defaults operationalise the "assume breach" posture that distinguishes ZTA from legacy perimeter models. Where traditional architectures default to an allow-by-default topology — granting internal entities unrestricted access and relying on blacklists and intrusion detection systems to identify and block anomalies — ZTA inverts this logic entirely. Every entity, whether human user, automated service, or infrastructure component, begins in a state of zero access and zero network visibility. Access is provisioned only after the Policy Engine has affirmatively evaluated the entity's identity, device posture, network context, and behavioural history against the applicable security policy (Turner & O'Connor, 2024). This inversion eliminates the class of vulnerabilities that arises when security mechanisms fail to enumerate all prohibited actions — an inherently incomplete task in heterogeneous networks where the space of possible actions is combinatorially vast and continuously evolving.

The fail-safe principle acquires particular architectural urgency in distributed, cloud-native, and agentic computing environments where component failures are not exceptional but expected. Recent analyses of autonomous agent communication frameworks demonstrate that fail-safe defaults must govern not only human access decisions but also machine-to-machine interactions: when an AI agent encounters an operational exception, loses connectivity to its policy authority, or receives a telemetry feed that violates expected parameters, the agent must default to its most restrictive operational posture rather than continuing under the authority of a stale or potentially compromised authorisation (Davidson, 2025). This requirement extends naturally to the SDP framework's treatment of controller unavailability — a scenario in which the fail-safe default mandates that all SDP Gateways revert to a fully closed state, denying all new connection requests until the controller is restored and the policy evaluation pipeline is re-established (Cloud Security Alliance, 2024).

However, a rigorous application of fail-safe defaults reveals a tension that neither NIST SP 800-207 nor the CSA SDP specification fully resolves: the operational cost of default denial under conditions of ambiguity. A strict fail-safe implementation denies access whenever the policy evaluation produces an indeterminate result — when evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or temporally stale. In heterogeneous enterprise networks where sensor failures, network partitions, and telemetry delays are operationally routine, a binary fail-safe mechanism produces a rate of access denials that is disproportionate to actual threat conditions, driving user frustration and incentivising workarounds that undermine the security architecture itself (Buck et al., 2022; Alawida et al., 2024). The Dempster-Shafer trust evaluation framework proposed in this thesis addresses this tension through its explicit representation of epistemic uncertainty. When evidence is insufficient to commit belief mass to either the Safe or Unsafe hypothesis, the framework does not default to a binary denial but instead assigns the uncommitted mass to the frame of discernment ($m(\Theta)$), routing the entity into a constrained access tier that is proportionate to the degree of uncertainty. This graduated fail-safe mechanism preserves the principle's security guarantee — no entity receives full access without affirmative evidence — while eliminating the operational friction of binary denial under ambiguous conditions.

## 15. The Principle of Least Privilege: Dynamic Privilege Scoping in Continuous Trust Architectures

The principle of least privilege, also formalised by Saltzer and Schroeder (1975) and subsequently codified as a core tenet of every major cybersecurity framework from the Orange Book (DoD, 1985) through NIST SP 800-207, mandates that every entity — user, process, or system component — be granted only the minimum set of privileges necessary to perform its currently authorised function, and only for the minimum duration required. The principle operates on two dimensions simultaneously: scope (the breadth of resources accessible) and duration (the temporal window during which access persists). A privilege grant that is minimally scoped but temporally unbounded violates least privilege just as surely as one that is appropriately time-bounded but excessively broad. The principle's purpose is to contain the blast radius of compromise: an entity that operates with minimal privileges provides an attacker who compromises it with correspondingly minimal leverage for lateral movement, privilege escalation, and data exfiltration (NIST, 2025; Alawida et al., 2024).

Within Zero Trust Architecture, least privilege is elevated from a discretionary best practice to an architectural mandate. NIST SP 800-207 explicitly requires that "access to individual enterprise resources is granted on a per-session basis" and that "access to a resource is determined by dynamic policy — including the observable state of client identity, application/service, and the requesting asset" (Rose et al., 2020, p. 6). This formulation integrates both the scope and duration dimensions of least privilege: per-session access prevents the accumulation of persistent privileges across sessions, while dynamic policy evaluation ensures that the scope of access reflects the entity's current contextual state rather than a static role assignment. Recent empirical analyses of zero trust deployments demonstrate that organisations implementing dynamic, context-aware least privilege — as opposed to static role-based assignments — achieve a 23% reduction in mean time to contain breaches and a 31% reduction in the volume of data exposed during incidents, attributable directly to the containment effect of minimally scoped, temporally bounded access grants (IBM Security, 2024; Smith & Jones, 2025).

The practical implementation of least privilege in heterogeneous enterprise networks, however, confronts a fundamental tension between granularity and manageability. In environments comprising thousands of resources, hundreds of user roles, and constantly shifting contextual conditions, defining and maintaining per-resource, per-context privilege policies imposes a combinatorial administrative burden that static policy management tools cannot sustain. The phenomenon of "privilege creep" — the gradual accumulation of access rights as employees transition between roles, projects, and organisational units without corresponding privilege revocation — is documented as one of the most prevalent and persistent access control vulnerabilities in enterprise environments, with recent surveys indicating that over 60% of enterprise users possess access rights that exceed their current functional requirements (Shin et al., 2025; CISA, 2024). This creep represents a systemic violation of least privilege that static RBAC, even when initially configured correctly, cannot prevent because it lacks the temporal and contextual mechanisms to automatically reduce privileges when the conditions that justified their grant no longer obtain.

The Ensemble Trust Model proposed in this thesis operationalises the temporal dimension of least privilege through its dual-horizon decay architecture. The short-term exponential decay window (30 minutes) ensures that the evidentiary weight of the initial authentication — and the privilege scope it justified — degrades rapidly, forcing re-evaluation before the session can persist beyond the compliance-mandated inactivity timeout derived from NIST SP 800-63B (Grassi et al., 2025). The long-term inertia window (48 hours) ensures that even entities with established behavioural baselines undergo complete privilege re-evaluation within a policy-defined maximum session lifetime. The scope dimension of least privilege is operationalised through the graduated trust threshold architecture: entities whose trust scores fall within the constrained access zone receive a dynamically narrowed privilege set — read-only access rather than read-write, access to non-sensitive resources rather than classified assets — that is proportionate to the residual trust score rather than determined by a static role assignment. This continuous, mathematically governed privilege scoping ensures that least privilege is not a point-in-time configuration but a dynamically enforced invariant that adapts in real time to changes in the entity's observed trustworthiness.

The interaction between least privilege and the Dempster-Shafer fusion engine introduces a further refinement absent from conventional access control models. When the fusion engine detects elevated uncertainty in a specific domain — for example, when device posture evidence is absent because the entity is a BYOD device without an enterprise agent — the engine does not uniformly restrict all privileges. Instead, the domain-specific uncertainty is mapped to domain-relevant privilege restrictions: uncertainty in device posture triggers restrictions on access to resources that are sensitive to device compromise (e.g., local data caching, peripheral device access), while preserving access to resources that are not device-dependent (e.g., cloud-hosted read-only dashboards). This domain-aware privilege scoping implements a contextually nuanced form of least privilege that treats privilege reduction as a targeted response to specific evidence gaps rather than a blanket restriction triggered by aggregate trust score degradation (Chen & Wang, 2025; Xu, 2024).

## 16. The Principle of Complete Mediation: Every Access Request as an Independent Trust Evaluation

The principle of complete mediation, the third foundational design criterion established by Saltzer and Schroeder (1975), requires that every access to every object within the system be checked against the access control policy — without exception, without caching of prior decisions, and without reliance on assumptions derived from previous evaluations. The principle is architecturally radical in its absolutism: it demands that the system treat each access request as an independent event, evaluating it against the current policy and current context regardless of whether an identical request was approved moments earlier. The rationale is that conditions change — credentials may be revoked, device states may degrade, threat intelligence may be updated, and policy rules may be modified — and any reliance on cached authorisation decisions creates a window during which the system operates under assumptions that may no longer hold (Saltzer & Schroeder, 1975).

Complete mediation is the design principle most directly aligned with the "never trust, always verify" mandate of Zero Trust Architecture. Where fail-safe defaults establish the ground state (default denial) and least privilege constrains the scope of granted access, complete mediation governs the temporal continuity of access evaluation — ensuring that authorisation is not a one-time event at session establishment but a continuous, uninterrupted process throughout the session lifecycle. NIST SP 800-207 embodies this principle through its requirement that "access to individual enterprise resources is granted on a per-session basis" and that the Policy Engine must "continuously monitor the state of all enterprise assets" to ensure that "the trust in the device and application may change over time" (Rose et al., 2020, pp. 6–7). The practical guidance published by NIST in SP 1800-35, which provides 19 reference implementations of Zero Trust Architecture, further operationalises complete mediation by demonstrating how continuous policy evaluation can be implemented using commercial and open-source technologies across diverse enterprise topologies (NIST, 2025).

The architectural challenge of implementing complete mediation at enterprise scale is not principally one of policy design but of computational performance. Evaluating every access request against a multi-domain policy that requires identity verification, device posture assessment, network context analysis, behavioural anomaly detection, and resource sensitivity classification imposes a per-request computational cost that, when multiplied by the volume of access events in a modern enterprise — millions of requests per hour across thousands of endpoints — produces an aggregate evaluation burden that can overwhelm centralised policy engines. Recent performance analyses of Zero Trust deployments report that naïve implementations of complete mediation, in which every access event triggers a full policy evaluation cycle, introduce latencies of 50–200 ms per decision that are incompatible with the real-time requirements of interactive applications, industrial control systems, and financial transaction processing (Oqaily et al., 2024). This performance constraint has historically forced enterprises to compromise on complete mediation by caching authorisation decisions for configurable durations — typically 5 to 15 minutes — during which subsequent access requests are approved without re-evaluation. Such caching directly violates the principle by creating a window of unmediated access that adversaries can exploit through session hijacking, credential replay, or device compromise occurring after the cached decision was made but before it expires.

The SDP framework's treatment of complete mediation illustrates this architectural tension. The SDP Join process is a rigorous implementation of complete mediation for the initial access event — the multi-phase verification workflow evaluates identity, device posture, and policy compliance before granting any access. However, once the SDP Gateway establishes the encrypted tunnel to the protected resource, subsequent data flows within that tunnel are not individually mediated. The tunnel remains open until an explicit Leave event (posture degradation detection, administrator revocation, or entitlement timeout) terminates it. During the tunnel's active lifetime, every packet traversing the tunnel is implicitly authorised by the initial Join decision — a violation of complete mediation that creates the implicit trust period exploited by session hijackers and persistent threat actors (Cloud Security Alliance, 2022; Chen et al., 2025).

The Dynamic Contextual Trust Architecture proposed in this thesis resolves this tension through a mechanism that achieves the security guarantees of complete mediation without imposing its full per-request computational cost. The Dempster-Shafer trust evaluation engine continuously computes a fused trust score from streaming multi-domain telemetry, updating the score at the telemetry polling interval (configurable from sub-second to 30-second granularity depending on the deployment's performance constraints and security requirements). This continuous score serves as an ambient mediator: rather than evaluating each individual access request independently against the full policy, the system maintains a continuously updated trust score that reflects the entity's current security posture at all times. Access decisions are then evaluated against this pre-computed, continuously refreshed trust score — a process that imposes negligible per-request latency because the computationally expensive fusion and decay calculations have already been performed asynchronously. When the trust score crosses a threshold boundary (full access → constrained → denied), the system immediately mediates all subsequent access by adjusting the entity's privilege scope or terminating the session entirely.

This architecture achieves what may be termed "amortised complete mediation" — the security property that every access is evaluated against a current contextual assessment, combined with the performance property that the expensive contextual assessment is computed continuously in the background rather than synchronously per request. The temporal decay function plays a critical role in this mechanism: by continuously depreciating the evidentiary weight of past authentication signals, the decay ensures that the trust score reflects only evidence that is temporally current, preventing the system from relying on stale evaluations. The mathematical guarantee is that no entity can maintain access for longer than the decay window's effective lifetime without generating fresh evidence of continued trustworthiness — a temporal bound on unmediated access that is architecturally enforceable regardless of whether explicit revocation events are detected (Shin et al., 2025; Davidson, 2025).

## 17. The Principle of Separation of Privilege: Multi-Domain Evidence as an Architectural Safeguard

The principle of separation of privilege, formalised by Saltzer and Schroeder (1975) as the fourth foundational design criterion for protection mechanisms, stipulates that a system should not grant access based on the satisfaction of a single condition. Instead, two or more independent conditions must be met before the protection mechanism permits the requested operation. The principle draws its rationale from an elementary observation in reliability engineering: requiring the concurrent satisfaction of *n* independent conditions reduces the probability of an unauthorised access to the product of the individual compromise probabilities, achieving exponential security amplification as additional conditions are introduced. A lock that requires two independent keys is not merely twice as secure as a single-key lock — it is categorically more secure, because an adversary must independently compromise both keys rather than either one (Saltzer & Schroeder, 1975). The principle's enduring influence is visible in its concrete manifestations across contemporary security practice: multi-factor authentication, dual-authorisation controls in financial systems, and the separation of duties mandated by compliance frameworks such as SOX, PCI DSS, and ISO 27001 all instantiate the separation of privilege in domain-specific operational contexts.

Within Zero Trust Architecture, the principle of separation of privilege finds its most architecturally consequential expression in the requirement for multi-dimensional trust evaluation. NIST SP 800-207 mandates that the Policy Engine evaluate access requests against multiple independent input variables — identity assurance, device posture, network context, behavioural analytics, resource sensitivity, and threat intelligence — rather than granting access on the basis of any single signal (Rose et al., 2020). This multi-input mandate is a direct architectural instantiation of separation of privilege: no single authentication factor, device compliance check, or network location signal is sufficient by itself to justify access. The entity must satisfy conditions across multiple independent evaluation dimensions before the Policy Engine issues a positive access determination. The CISA Zero Trust Maturity Model v2.1 further operationalises this principle by defining maturity stages across five distinct pillars — Identity, Devices, Networks, Applications and Workloads, and Data — and requiring that organisations advance across all pillars rather than achieving depth in a single dimension while neglecting others (CISA, 2024). This pillar-based maturity model encodes the separation of privilege principle at the organisational level, ensuring that the overall security posture cannot be sustained by strength in one domain alone.

However, both NIST SP 800-207 and the CSA SDP framework implement separation of privilege in a structurally limited manner that does not fully exploit the principle's architectural potential. In the SDP Join process, the multi-phase verification workflow evaluates identity and device posture as sequential, independent checks — an entity must pass both the identity authentication phase and the device posture evaluation phase before receiving an entitlement. This sequential gating implements separation of privilege at the session establishment boundary: neither identity alone nor device posture alone is sufficient for access (Cloud Security Alliance, 2024). Yet once the phases are passed and the entitlement is issued, the separation collapses. During the active session, the SDP framework does not continuously re-evaluate multiple independent conditions; it monitors for discrete posture degradation events through the SDP Client, collapsing the multi-dimensional evaluation into a single-channel monitoring dependency. If the SDP Client is compromised, disabled, or unable to communicate with the Controller, the separation of privilege that existed at session establishment is effectively neutralised — the entitlement persists under conditions where the independent verification channels that justified it are no longer operative.

NIST SP 800-207's treatment of the Trust Algorithm similarly acknowledges separation of privilege in principle but leaves its mathematical implementation unspecified. The framework identifies multiple input variables that the Policy Engine should consider but provides no normative guidance on how these variables should be combined, weighted, or correlated to produce an access decision. In practice, many Zero Trust implementations reduce the multi-input evaluation to a simple weighted sum or rule-based threshold that assigns a fixed score to each input variable and grants access when the aggregate exceeds a predetermined threshold (Xu, 2024; Shin et al., 2025). Such linear aggregation, while computationally efficient, fundamentally undermines separation of privilege: a sufficiently high score in one domain can compensate for a deficient score in another, violating the principle's requirement that multiple independent conditions must each be satisfactorily met. An entity with excellent identity assurance but severely degraded device posture should not receive the same access as an entity that satisfies both conditions — yet a weighted-sum aggregation permits precisely this compensatory behaviour.

The Dempster-Shafer evidential fusion architecture proposed in this thesis provides the mathematically principled operationalisation of separation of privilege that both NIST and SDP frameworks lack. The architecture evaluates four independent telemetry domains — Identity, Device, Network, and Application/Data — as separate evidence sources, each producing its own basic probability assignment (BPA) over the binary frame of discernment $\Theta = \{Safe, Unsafe\}$. Critically, the Dempster-Shafer combination rule does not perform weighted averaging; it performs conjunctive fusion that requires concordant evidence across sources to produce high committed belief. When two domains provide concordant evidence of safety, the fused belief in $\{Safe\}$ is amplified beyond what either source alone could produce. When one domain provides evidence of safety while another provides evidence of risk, the combination rule produces elevated conflict ($\kappa$), which the variance-based weight suppression mechanism routes into uncommitted mass ($m(\Theta)$) rather than allowing the safe evidence to compensate for the unsafe evidence. This conjunctive property ensures that no single domain's favourable assessment can override genuine risk signals from another domain — a mathematically enforceable implementation of separation of privilege that operates continuously throughout the session lifetime rather than only at the establishment boundary.

The interaction between separation of privilege and the temporal decay mechanism introduces a further architectural dimension. Because each domain's evidence is independently subject to exponential decay, the separation of privilege is not merely spatial (across domains) but also temporal (across time). An entity that established strong identity assurance at session initiation but whose device posture has since degraded does not retain the benefit of the initial identity evaluation to compensate for the current device deficiency. The identity evidence, though originally strong, has been temporally discounted, and the current fused trust score reflects only the residual weight of decayed identity evidence combined with the current device posture signal. This temporal separation ensures that the principle cannot be circumvented by establishing strong credentials in one domain at time $t = 0$ and subsequently allowing other domains to degrade during the session — a temporal attack vector that static separation-of-privilege implementations, which evaluate conditions only at the access decision point, cannot detect (Chen & Wang, 2025; Davidson, 2025).

The variance-based dynamic weighting mechanism further reinforces separation of privilege by preventing a single high-confidence domain from dominating the fused trust score. Domains that exhibit low variance — stable, consistent telemetry signals — receive higher weighting in the fusion process, while domains exhibiting high variance receive suppressed weighting regardless of their absolute score. This variance sensitivity ensures that an attacker who spoofs a single domain to report consistently high trust cannot leverage that domain's artificial stability to compensate for genuine risk signals from other domains. The mathematical effect is that each domain must independently demonstrate both favourable assessment and signal stability before its evidence can materially influence the fused trust score — a dual condition within each domain that instantiates separation of privilege at the intra-domain level as well as the inter-domain level.

The interaction among the four principles — fail-safe defaults, least privilege, complete mediation, and separation of privilege — establishes a coherent and mutually reinforcing architectural foundation for the Ensemble Trust Model. Fail-safe defaults establish denial as the ground state, ensuring that the architecture is secure by construction. Least privilege constrains the scope and duration of any access grant, minimising the attack surface available to compromised entities. Complete mediation ensures that access authorisation is continuously current, preventing adversaries from exploiting temporal gaps between evaluations. Separation of privilege ensures that no single evidence source, domain, or temporal snapshot is sufficient to justify access, requiring concordant satisfaction of multiple independent conditions. Together, these four principles define the invariants that the Dempster-Shafer fusion engine, the temporal decay functions, the variance-based weighting, and the graduated threshold architecture are mathematically designed to enforce — transforming classical security design criteria into computationally verifiable properties of the dynamic trust evaluation system.
