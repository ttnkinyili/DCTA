# Chapter: Evaluation of Trust Models in Zero Trust Architectures

## 1.1 Introduction
The fundamental premise of Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) is the elimination of implicit trust. Traditional access control models have historically relied on static, perimeter-based defenses where authentication is a discrete event granting durable access. However, in modern heterogeneous environments—characterized by remote work, Bring Your Own Device (BYOD) policies, and ephemeral cloud infrastructures—the context of a user and their device is highly volatile. A device authenticated as secure at one moment may be compromised minutes later.

This chapter presents a comprehensive evaluation of computational trust models, moving from legacy static paradigms to advanced dynamic fusion architectures. We analyze the theoretical and operational implications of six distinct access models: Implicit Trust (No Policy), Single-Domain Criteria Trust, Multidomain Score-Based Trust (Base DS Model), and three advanced Multidomain variations incorporating temporal dynamics (Linear Time, Exponential Time, and Ensemble Inertia). The evaluation explores the structural tension between absolute security verification and operational usability, culminating in a stateful behavioral engine that continuously evaluates risk.

## 2. Model Progression and Theoretical Evaluation

### 2.1 The Imperative for Policy: Mitigating Implicit Trust Vulnerabilities

The absence of rigorous, context-aware access policies—often manifesting as "Implicit Trust" or "No Policy" models following an initial perimeter breach—creates an existential vulnerability within modern, heterogeneous enterprise environments. Classic "castle-and-moat" architectures operate on the flawed assumption that authentication at the boundary equates to continuous authorization within the interior. In such open-policy ecosystems, once an attacker compromises a single endpoint (e.g., via a stolen VPN credential or a phishing payload on a remote employee's device), they are granted unchecked lateral mobility. Without continuous verification or micro-segmentation policies dictating *what* a specific user can access and *when*, this implicit trust becomes the primary vector for rapid malware propagation. Ransomware operations exploit these flat network topologies to automatically traverse horizontally, encrypting critical assets across servers and workstations that should have been mathematically isolated from the initial point of ingress (IBM Security, 2024).

Furthermore, open access policies severely exacerbate the risk and financial impact of internal data misuse and exfiltration. In environments lacking granular authorization thresholds, both malicious insiders and compromised external actors operate with a surplus of unsanctioned privileges. The inability to dynamically restrict access based on shifting contextual risk (e.g., a sudden login from an anomalous location or via an unpatched device) allows attackers ample time to locate and siphon sensitive data undetected. The financial necessity of dismantling implicit trust is stark; organizations employing mature Zero Trust architectures—which inherently demand strict, continuous policy enforcement—have demonstrated an ability to reduce the lifecycle of a breach and lower the associated financial damages by over $1 million per incident compared to those relying on legacy, open-trust paradigms (Gartner, 2023; IBM Security, 2024). Thus, enforcing a continuous, mathematically justified policy tiering system is not merely an exercise in theoretical security, but a quantifiable requirement for operational survival against automated modern threats.

### 2.2 Single Criteria: The Fragility of Isolated Domain Verification

While an improvement over entirely open networks, access control models reliant on single-domain criteria (such as evaluating *only* device posture or *only* network location) remain fundamentally fragile in the face of modern enterprise threats. The core limitation of a single-factor approach is its lack of contextual depth; it inherently trusts a single axis of verification while remaining blind to systemic compromises occurring in adjacent domains. For example, if an architecture grants access based solely on a valid, uncompromised remote access gateway (e.g., a VPN connection without secondary hardware checks), an adversary who successfully executes a credential stuffing or phishing attack can immediately inherit that trust. Recent incident analyses have demonstrated that adversaries frequently exploit these single-point-of-verification architectures to bypass initial defenses, leveraging the established trust in one domain to facilitate rapid lateral movement and deploy ransomware throughout the wider environment (Breached Company Reports, 2025).

This structural myopia dramatically escalates the likelihood of data misuse and resource hijacking. A single-domain model cannot reconcile conflicting contextual signals—such as a valid identity token attempting to download massive volumes of sensitive data from a geographically anomalous IP address. Because the system's policy engine only verifies the identity token (the single criteria), the anomalous, potentially malicious behavioral context is ignored. This failure to correlate multi-vector telemetry allows attackers to "live off the land" undetected, utilizing legitimate administrative credentials for unauthorized data exfiltration or resource manipulation (Elastic Security Labs, 2024). To effectively mitigate these risks, modern Zero Trust frameworks must transcend single-domain checks, evolving toward continuous, multidomain verification engines that dynamically analyze identity, device health, network context, and application behavior in tandem.

### 2.3 Hierarchical Multi-domain Policy: Complexities and Exploitation Vectors

While a marked improvement over single-domain architectures, Hierarchical Multi-domain Policy (HMP) introduces its own set of structural vulnerabilities when deployed in modern, heterogeneous enterprise environments. HMP architectures inherently rely on a rigid, top-down derivation of trust, where macro-level security postures cascade down to discrete micro-domains (e.g., a corporate root policy dictating the access constraints of an isolated cloud enclave). However, recent analyses demonstrate that this hierarchical rigidity struggles to adapt to the ephemeral nature of modern cloud workloads and remote access scenarios. Because HMP systems often enforce static rules that lag behind real-time contextual shifts, adversaries frequently exploit the operational delay between micro-domain state changes and macro-policy updates. If an attacker compromises a lower-tier domain—such as an improperly configured microservice or an edge IoT device—they can leverage the inherent trust pathways designed for inter-domain communication to bypass the broader security hierarchy, facilitating covert malware propagation (Cloud Security Alliance, 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

Furthermore, the complexity of orchestrating hierarchical trust across distributed cloud infrastructures often leads to severe policy misconfigurations, dramatically increasing the surface area for data and resource misuse. In an effort to maintain operational continuity across disparate domains, network administrators frequently implement overly permissive inter-domain routing rules, creating fatal security gaps. When a malicious actor or an insider threat gains access to a federated domain characterized by these loose hierarchical bonds, they can exploit the systemic administrative overhead to manipulate access privileges, enabling the lateral exfiltration of sensitive datasets via trusted third-party APIs or supply chain vectors. As advanced, AI-augmented threats continue to target these structural seams within complex policy matrices, it becomes evident that rigid hierarchical models must evolve into fully dynamic, continuously evaluated contextual fabrics to prevent catastrophic data breaches (Al-Sanjary et al., 2023; Cloud Security Alliance, 2025).


### 2.4 Multi-Domain with Static Trust Scores and Domain Weights: The Peril of Inflexible Calibration

The transition to a Multi-Domain architecture represents a necessary evolution in ZTA, yet implementations that depend on static trust scores and fixed domain weights often recreate the very vulnerabilities they sought to eliminate. In these theoretically advanced models, an entity is evaluated across multiple axes (e.g., identity, device health, location), but a static score is calculated and maintained for the duration of a session, or the relative importance (weight) of each domain remains rigid regardless of environmental context. This inflexibility is fundamentally misaligned with the dynamic nature of both operational networks and modern cyber threats. For instance, if a user is granted a high static trust score based on a secure initial handshake from a corporate device, that score acts as a durational passport. If that same device is subsequently infected with a zero-day payload or its network connection is subtly hijacked, the static policy engine lacks the temporal awareness to trigger a re-evaluation. The malware can then inherit the user's high trust score, propagating laterally across domains under the guise of legitimate, pre-approved traffic (Ahmed et al., 2024; National Institutes of Health [NIH], 2023).

Moreover, the reliance on static domain weights severely hampers an enterprise's ability to mitigate sophisticated data misuse and insider threats. In an adaptive model, the weight of a geographic location signal might dramatically increase if the user attempts to access highly classified data outside of standard business hours. Conversely, a static weighting system forces the policy engine to treat context identically regardless of the specific resource being requested or the underlying behavioral volatility. This rigidity empowers compromised identities or malicious insiders; knowing that their assigned trust score will not degrade based on anomalous data access patterns, adversaries can execute slow, methodical "low and slow" exfiltration campaigns. Because the system is not continuously recalibrating trust based on dynamic behavioral deviations (such as an unprecedented volume of database queries), the enterprise remains highly susceptible to data loss disguised as authorized operational activity. To counter this, empirical research overwhelmingly advocates for the abolition of static scoring in favor of continuous, AI-driven contextual re-evaluations and dynamic weight adjustments (Alsubhi et al., 2024; NIH, 2023).

### 2.5 Dynamic Weighting with Weighted Belief Fusion: Contextual Resilience

To resolve the catastrophic limitations of static trust, advanced security architectures employ **Dynamic Variance-Based Weighting** coupled with **Dempster-Shafer (DS) Belief Fusion**. In this algorithmic approach, trust is not a static integer but a continuously evolving mathematical mass. As demonstrated by the `dynamic_trust_weighting.py` and `weighted_belief_fusion.py` engines, this model continuously tracks the historical variance of individual telemetry parameters (e.g., network anomalies, application consistency). When a domain exhibits high variance—indicative of erratic behavior, a compromised sensor, or an active intrusion—the algorithm mathematically discounts that domain's influence by assigning it a lower dynamic weight. This weight is subsequently converted into a Dempster-Shafer mass function defining *Belief* (Safe), *Disbelief* (Unsafe), and critically, *Uncertainty* (1.0 - Weight). By explicitly modeling mathematical uncertainty, the fusion engine refuses to grant implicit trust to unstable signals. If malware begins propagating, creating anomalous network spikes, the variance skyrockets, the network domain weight plummets, and the overall fused *Belief (Safe)* score drops precipitously, thereby severing lateral routing pathways before the infection can spread across micro-segments (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023).

This dynamic, multi-domain fusion is exceptionally lethal to insider threats and data misuse campaigns. Malicious insiders typically possess valid identities and operate from compliant devices, easily bypassing static access controls. However, when an insider deviates from their normal baseline to siphon sensitive resources, this erratic action spikes their behavioral variance. The fusion engine interprets this sudden instability by increasing the *Uncertainty* mass associated with their identity or application domain. Rather than making a binary "Allow/Deny" decision based on a stolen password, the Dempster-Shafer engine proportionately degrades the user's aggregated trust score from "Full Access" down to "Limited Access" or "No Access." This continuous, context-aware mathematical degradation effectively quarantines compromised identities or anomalous workloads in real-time, preventing the unauthorized exfiltration of critical enterprise datasets even when the initial authentication vectors remain cryptographically valid (Chen et al., 2024; PWC, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025).

### 2.6 Fused Dynamic Weighting with Temporal Decay: Enforcing Session Ephemerality

While dynamic variance-based weighting excels at quarantining erratic behavior, it remains vulnerable to sophisticated adversaries executing "low and slow" operations or deploying dormant malware that perfectly mimics standard user activity. If an attacker successfully hijacks an established session and maintains a low behavioral variance, the spatial-only fusion model (analyzed in Section 5) may inadvertently sustain their high trust score indefinitely. To address this critical blind spot, advanced architectures integrate **Temporal Decay**—specifically exponential time decay—directly into the fused trust calculation. As demonstrated in the algorithmic evolution from `weighted_belief_fusion.py` to `weighted_belief_fusion_2.py`, this model explicitly tracks "Session Aging" and applies a mathematical depreciation factor to the aggregated *Belief (Safe)* score. By treating trust as a rapidly depreciating asset rather than a durable state, the system enforces strict session ephemerality; regardless of how stable or "normal" the telemetry appears, the user's overarching trust score will inexorably degrade toward "Limited Access" or "No Access" as time progresses (Elkhatib et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2023).

This integration of temporal physics establishes an absolute containment boundary against persistent intrusions and data misuse in heterogeneous enterprise networks. For malware designed to spread laterally by piggybacking on authorized connections over long durations, the exponential decay function acts as a definitive kill switch. Even if the malware evades detection by all spatial security controls (identity, device posture, network anomaly detection), the temporal decay factor will unilaterally sever its access rights by exhausting the session's mathematical validity. This forces a mandatory re-authentication and re-verification event, resetting the baseline and increasing the likelihood of exposing the hidden payload during the renewed cryptographic challenge. Consequently, the hybridization of spatial uncertainty (variance) with temporal expiration (decay) provides the most mathematically resilient defense against both noisy, fast-propagating malware and stealthy, patient insider exfiltration attempts (Li & Wang, 2025; RSA Security, 2024).

### 2.7 Fused Dynamic Weighting with Temporal Decay (Linear): Proportional Session Degradation

While Section 5 demonstrates the efficacy of spatial variance in immediately quarantining volatile and erratic behavior, it inherently trusts stable sessions. If a compromised insider or dormant malware mimics normal behavior perfectly (maintaining low variance), the spatial-only model could theoretically grant indefinite access. To mitigate this, integrating a **Linear Temporal Decay** factor ($D(t) = 1 - t/T_{session}$) introduces a steady, completely predictable degradation of the fused trust score over time. Unlike the spatial model, linear temporal decay transforms trust into a constantly depreciating asset. Even if the user’s telemetry remains absolutely stable and within standard operational bounds, the system mathematically erodes their aggregated *Belief (Safe)* score. This predictable degradation guarantees that the window of opportunity for lateral malware propagation or "low and slow" data exfiltration is strictly finite; all sessions, regardless of their behavioral purity, will eventually hit the revocation threshold, forcing a complete cryptographic re-authentication (Elkhatib et al., 2024).

The distinct operational advantage of linear temporal decay over purely spatial models is the establishment of a **Proportional Time-To-Live (TTL)**. Because trust degrades at a constant mathematical rate, the actual duration a session remains active (the "effective session length") becomes directly dependent on the initial spatial security posture. An authentication event originating from a highly secure, managed corporate environment begins with a substantial "trust buffer" (e.g., $T_{spatial} \approx 0.95$); the linear decay will take a significantly longer time to erode this score below the critical thresholds (such as 0.45 for No Access). Conversely, an entity connecting from a high-risk public Wi-Fi network starts with a much smaller trust buffer. The linear decay rapidly exhausts this limited surplus, resulting in an exceptionally brief session window. This algorithmic mechanism ensures that riskier contextual environments mathematically cannot sustain long-lived sessions, thereby suffocating the temporal runway necessary for sophisticated intrusions and resource misuse in heterogeneous networks (PWC, 2024; Smith et al., 2023).

### 2.8 Fused Dynamic Weighting with Temporal Decay (Exponential): Aggressive Session Expiration

While the linear model ensures eventual revocation, its steady, constant rate of trust degradation may not be aggressive enough for highly sensitive zero-trust enclaves. When confronting rapidly propagating automated malware or sophisticated adversaries attempting immediate exfiltration upon hijacking a session, any delay in trust revocation is a fatal liability. To combat this, ultra-secure implementations utilize an **Exponential Temporal Decay** function—as defined in `dynamic_trust_weighting_time_exp.py` utilizing $D(t) = e^{-\lambda (t/T)}$. Unlike the gradual slope of linear decay, exponential decay inflicts a precipitous initial drop in trust. The moment an entity halts active verification, the mathematical mass representing its "Session Freshness" evaporates rapidly. Because the decay operates on an exponential curve rather than a linear track, the *Belief (Safe)* score plummets dramatically in the early phases of the session before asymptoting near zero. This forces immediate and constant re-verification; if a malware payload attempts to move laterally just minutes after a legitimate user authentication, the exponential decay will have already stripped away the high-trust buffer, shifting the session into a "Limited Access" or revoked state and freezing the lateral connection attempt (Robbins et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

The operational deployment of exponential decay represents a profound paradigm shift: it transitions the network from a state of "default trust with eventual expiration" to a state of **"continuous algorithmic suspicion."** For malicious insiders, this mechanism is highly disruptive. An insider performing a "low and slow" data misuse campaign relies on a durably stable trust score across a session to camouflage their unauthorized queries. Under an exponential decay model, the user's base trust evaporates so aggressively that they cannot sustain high-privilege access without triggering continuous, frictionless re-authentication challenges (e.g., biometric prompts or behavioral re-evaluations). If the insider fails these micro-challenges due to anomalous tooling or unusual geographic location, the already-decayed trust score collapses completely, paralyzing the exfiltration attempt. Empirical studies on advanced persistent threats (APTs) demonstrate that while linear decay reduces the attack window, exponential decay fundamentally shatters it, imposing an untenable mathematical friction on any adversary attempting to persist within the enterprise perimeter (Robbins et al., 2025; RSA Security, 2024).

### 2.9 Fused Dynamic Weighting Ensemble: Balancing Freshness and Historic Inertia

While aggressive temporal models successfully eradicate "implicit trust periods," over-indexing on immediate sensory data or exponential decay creates severe usability and continuity challenges in operational enterprise environments. If trust evaporates entirely upon a momentary telemetry hiccup or brief idle period, legitimate users face endless friction. To resolve the tension between absolute security and operational continuity, the pinnacle of adaptive access is the **Ensemble Trust Model** (detailed in `ensemble_trust_simulator.py`). This architecture hybridizes short-term spatial fusion (data freshness) with a long-term temporal baseline (historic inertia). The algorithm utilizes a weighted mathematical mixture where instantaneous telemetry (the "Freshness Component," governed by a rapid 30-minute decay) is fused with the user’s long-standing behavioral baseline (the "Inertia Component," governed by a slow 48-hour decay). At the initiation of a session, access is granted entirely based on fresh cryptographic proofs. As the session matures, the influence of the instantaneous handshake dynamically fades, and the system increasingly relies on the historic stability of the user's behavior to maintain the connection. This prevents a momentary drop in network signal (ambient noise) from severing a highly trusted executive's connection during a critical operation (Gartner, 2024; IBM Security, 2025).

Crucially, this Ensemble approach leverages the mathematical momentum of "Inertia" to counter sophisticated malware propagation and session hijacking attempting to masquerade as legitimate behavior. If an attacker steals a valid authentication token mid-session, they lack the corresponding historic behavioral inertia of the targeted user. Even if their stolen token satisfies the short-term mathematical checks, the moment the attacker deviates from the victim's long-term behavior patterns (e.g., executing unfamiliar queries, opening new administrative ports), the "Inertia Component" collapses. Because the Ensemble engine dynamically mixes these two time horizons, anomalous actions by the adversary instantly corrupt the historic baseline, causing the fused trust score to plunge below the acceptable threshold. Rather than relying solely on a single aggressive decay timer or a single spatial snapshot, the Ensemble model effectively traps malicious actors in a continuous paradox: to maintain access, they must successfully compromise the short-term authentication challenge while simultaneously reproducing the exact long-term behavioral cadence of the victim over an extended duration, a hurdle that effectively neutralizes both fast-moving malware and persistent insider data misuse (Al-Tariq et al., 2025; Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency [CISA], 2024).

## 3. Discussion and Conclusion: The Evolution to Continuous Algorithmic Suspicion

The progression of computational trust architectures evaluated in this document underscores a fundamental reality of modern cybersecurity: security state cannot rely on absolute, discrete proofs. The analysis reveals that static frameworks—whether they consist of entirely open "No Policy" perimeters or more granular single-domain checks—are intrinsically fragile constructs. They operate on binary logic, granting durable trust post-authentication and remaining structurally blind to the nuanced degradation of context over time. This architectural myopia makes static systems acutely vulnerable to zero-day payloads, ransomware propagation, and session hijacking, as adversaries can simply inherit unexpired trust scores or exploit overly permissive routing rules intended for legitimate users (Elastic Security Labs, 2024; IBM Security, 2024). Attempts to resolve these threats utilizing static Multi-Domain policies fail for similar reasons; while they collect more context, they do not mathematically penalize the subsequent *variability* or *duration* of that behavior, empowering sophisticated insiders to execute "low and slow" data exfiltration campaigns under established identities.

The transition toward fully dynamic, multidomain fusion engines natively resolves the brittle nature of binary access controls. By leveraging algorithmic elements like variance-based weighting and Dempster-Shafer evidential theory, zero-trust systems transition from simple gatekeepers into probabilistic behavioral evaluators. Instead of issuing complete lockouts based on transient environmental noise, these engines calculate localized *Uncertainty*, routing users experiencing instability into safely quarantined "Limited Access" configurations (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023). However, spatial fusion alone remains incomplete without recognizing that trust is inherently an ephemeral, depreciating asset. The integration of Linear and Exponential temporal decay functions proves that limiting the architectural "time-to-live" mathematically suffocates the operational runway required by persistent threats and dormant malware (Li & Wang, 2025). 

Ultimately, the hybridization of highly sensitive short-term verification parameters with stable, long-term historical inertia—as demonstrated by the **Ensemble Trust Model**—provides the most robust blueprint for next-generation enterprise security. This model elegantly balances the tension between aggressive security logic and seamless operational usability. By trapping adversaries within a framework that demands both real-time cryptographic proof and perfect, sustained behavioral mimicry over significant temporal expanses, the architecture successfully transitions the enterprise network from a flawed "trust but verify" standard to a mathematically sound state of continuous algorithmic suspicion (Al-Tariq et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

### 3.1 Discussion_detail: The Limitation of Spatial-Only Models

In the context of the document, "spatial-only models" refers to systems that evaluate trust based purely on where the user is and what they are using, without considering the passage of time.

Let's break it down:

1. **Spatial (Space/Context):** This refers to the physical or logical "space" of the connection. For example: Are you on the corporate Wi-Fi? (Network space). Are you using a company-issued laptop? (Device space). Are you accessing classified files? (Data space).
2. **The "Only" Limitation:** A "spatial-only" model looks at all these factors at the exact moment you log in, calculates a score, and says, "Yes, you are safe." However, it stops there. Because it only looks at the space, it assumes that if you were safe at 9:00 AM, you are still safe at 2:00 PM, even if your context secretly changed (e.g., someone stole your laptop while you were at a coffee shop).

**In summary:** The paper refers to the "Base DS Model" as a "spatial-only model" because it perfectly fuses the signals from the Network, Device, App, and Data at a single point in time, but it lacks an understanding of temporal decay (the idea that trust should expire or degrade as the session gets older). This is why the paper then introduces the Time, Exponential Time, and Ensemble models—to add the missing dimension of Time to the Spatial evaluation.


The evaluation of the baseline Multidomain model highlights a critical algorithmic boundary: the "Base DS Model" is a **spatial-only model**. 

In this architecture, trust is fused perfectly across logical and physical "spaces"—evaluating *where* the user is connecting from (Network Domain), *what* hardware they are using (Device Domain), and *which* data they are accessing (Data Domain). However, this evaluation occurs at a single, static point in time (the cryptographic handshake). 

Because it lacks an independent temporal dimension, a spatial-only model assumes that if the mathematical context was verified as safe at 9:00 AM, it remains equally safe at 2:00 PM. It is blind to the reality that context degrades; a device may be compromised, or a network hijacked, long after the initial auth-token was issued. 

This spatial limitation is the driver behind the introduction of the Time, Exponential Time, and Ensemble variations—each designed to add the missing dimension of **Time** to the **Spatial** evaluation matrix.

### 3.2 Discussion_detail: Contextual Gray-Area Routing

A pivotal advancement demonstrated by the Multidomain models—particularly those utilizing Dempster-Shafer evidential fusion—is the capability for **Contextual Gray-Area Routing**. 

In traditional perimeter or single-domain security models, access decisions are strictly binary: a user is either granted 100% Full Access or 0% No Access. This black-and-white approach performs poorly in heterogeneous environments where sensors are prone to noise, jitter, and transient failures. 

**Contextual Gray-Area Routing** resolves this by operating on probability rather than rigid boolean logic:

1.  **Contextual Evaluation**: The architecture evaluates the holistic state of the session across multiple independent domains (Network, Data, Device, Application) simultaneously. It intrinsically understands that a compromised device on a secure network is a different *context* than a secure device on a compromised network.
2.  **Mapping the Gray Area**: When the evidentiary engine detects instability or conflicting signals (e.g., strong device health but anomalous network routing), it does not immediately default to a catastrophic lockout. Instead, it mathematically calculates the localized uncertainty (the "gray area").
3.  **Adaptive Routing**: Based on this calculated uncertainty, the system intelligently *routes* the user's connection to an appropriate, proportional trust tier—such as **"Limited Access."** 

By doing so, the architecture ensures that users experiencing transient environmental noise remain productive within a safely restricted state, rather than suffering total disruption. It confines binary "lockouts" only to sessions exhibiting mathematically verifiable maliciousness or complete systemic failure, radically improving both the security posture and the underlying user experience.
